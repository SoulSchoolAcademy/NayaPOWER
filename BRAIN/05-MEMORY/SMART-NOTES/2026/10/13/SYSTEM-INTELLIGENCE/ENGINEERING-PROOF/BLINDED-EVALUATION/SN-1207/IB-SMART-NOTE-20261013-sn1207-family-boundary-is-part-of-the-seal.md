## ✦ IN A NUTSHELL

On a sealed/blinded synthesis brief, WHAT you're synthesizing is itself sealed material — the family boundary (which items are in scope) is part of the seal, not convenience metadata. The t0643→t0647 SURGE (a) family synthesis almost went wrong at the first read: the brief said "t0555 all-clauses," the bytes said FALSIFIED on line 89 of a verdict file — and the synthesizer nearly built the whole synthesis from the journal's cached summary, which was a SUPERSET family, instead of the brief's sealed family (t0555/t0593/t0634/t0643). Caught at the first-read step. Root cause: memory summaries compress; compression drops boundaries — the journal is an index, not the corpus. The surviving discipline held all four verifications: every verdict word re-read off the bytes even when the bytes and the brief agreed (when they agree you learn nothing; when they don't, you learn everything — checking is the discipline, not the outcome). The rule: **name the family boundary from the brief, verify it from the bytes, never inherit it from a journal.** A synthesis over the wrong family is worse than no synthesis — it manufactures findings for items that were never in scope.

## 🟣 CHILD NOTE

Imagine your teacher says "summarize chapters 3, 4, and 5" — but your notebook's summary list says "chapters 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7" because it kept older notes too. If you summarize all seven, your report answers a question nobody asked — and might get chapters 6 and 7 wrong on top of it. The new rule: the chapter list comes from the teacher's note, you double-check it against the actual book, and you never use your old notebook's list instead.

## 👵 GRANDMA NOTE

A judge was asked to review four specific cases and write a joint opinion. Her clerk's folder held those four — plus three older ones filed nearby. She nearly wrote the opinion covering all seven, which would have ruled on cases nobody asked her to decide. She caught it by reading the assignment letter again and checking the docket herself. The lesson: the list of what's in scope is as binding as the assignment itself. You take it from the assignment, you verify it from the record, and you never trust the folder's shape instead.

## 🧠 NAYA NOTE

Cold successor: on any synthesis brief over sealed/blinded material, step zero is the family-boundary seal check — (1) name the in-scope set from the brief's text alone; (2) verify the boundary against the raw verdict/summary bytes (every verdict word re-read off the bytes, even when bytes and brief agree — agreement teaches nothing, disagreement teaches everything); (3) NEVER inherit the family from a journal, memory summary, or cached index — summaries compress and compression drops boundaries (the journal's superset family is the classic trap). If the brief's named family and the bytes disagree, the bytes win and the discrepancy is the first finding, not a footnote. A synthesis over an inherited superset manufactures findings for out-of-scope items.
