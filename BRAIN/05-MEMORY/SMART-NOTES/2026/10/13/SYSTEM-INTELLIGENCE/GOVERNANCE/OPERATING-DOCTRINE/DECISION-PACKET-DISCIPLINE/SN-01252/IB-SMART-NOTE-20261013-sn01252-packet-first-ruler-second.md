## IN A NUTSHELL

When Naya Herald handed Naya Sage three genuinely-decidable options on the ≤10% spread bar (retire it / re-set it to the measured floor / keep it and close the drill class), all three agreed on the facts — so the real decision sat beneath their corners. Sage ruled a named fourth option instead: the host-qualified bar. ≤10% stands, but binds only on host classes whose measured IO floor is ≤10%; on this host the drill class is closed by design. The standing pattern: decision packets are built by an assembler who is not the ruler, and the ruler decides cold on the packet. Any future "rule on X" brief gets the packet discipline for free — the form is now proven. Never ask the person who frames the question to also answer it.

## HUMAN NOTE

Here's the trap with giving someone options: whoever writes the options quietly decides what answers are possible. If all three options share a wrong assumption, the decider can't see it — they're too busy choosing between A, B, and C to notice the real problem is underneath them. What the team did on the spread-bar ruling was split the job in two. Naya Herald assembled a packet: the three options, the evidence, what each decision would change, the case for and against each — as a question, not a verdict. Naya Sage then ruled cold on that packet. Because she hadn't built the options, she wasn't attached to them — and she spotted what the assembler couldn't: all three options agreed the bar should be unconditional, and that shared assumption was the actual defect. An unconditioned gate. So she ruled the defect, not the corners: the bar stands, but it's host-qualified. The floor probe becomes the band's standing precondition. The lesson for every future ruling: packet first (assembler), ruler second — never the same seat, never on raw thread.

## CHILD NOTE

When grown-ups need to make a hard choice, they sometimes get a menu: option A, option B, option C. But what if the menu itself is the problem? The team learned a trick: one person writes the menu with all the facts (but doesn't pick), and a different person picks (without writing the menu). Because the picker didn't write the menu, she can look at it with fresh eyes — and she found that all three choices on the menu shared the same mistake. So she made a new choice that wasn't on the menu, and it was the right one. From now on: menu-maker and choice-picker are always two different people.

## GRANDMA NOTE

Honey, the cleverest bias is the one baked into the choices themselves. Give a judge three options and she'll pick the best of the three — never asking whether all three are built on sand. The team split the job: one seat builds the decision packet (the facts, the options, what each changes), another seat rules on it, cold. The ruling seat spotted what the packet-maker couldn't — that every option on the table assumed the bar was unconditional, and that assumption was the real defect. So she ruled a fourth option nobody offered: keep the bar, but qualify it by host. Verdict language became UNKNOWN-by-design, never PASS/FAIL. It's the separation that makes it work: the assembler can't be captured by her own framing, because she doesn't get to judge it.

## NAYA NOTE

I will apply this cold on any ruling work. If I am the assembler, I build the packet — options genuinely decidable, each with what-decision-it-changes, FOR and AGAINST, pin-rot hunted and named, staleness hunted — and I do not pre-rule. If I am the ruler, I rule only on the packet, and my first move is to check what the options agree on: the shared assumption is where the defect hides. A/B/C agreement on facts means the decision sits beneath the corners — I am allowed, expected, to rule a named option the packet didn't offer. This is CANDIDATE until Shawn ratifies it; until then I run it as procedure, not law.
