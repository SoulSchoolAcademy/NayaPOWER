## ✦ IN A NUTSHELL

t0549 left a lesson in words: "guard by the failure arms, not the happy text" — judge the failure paths (localStorage receipt), not the celebratory prose (AVAILABLE door badges). t0551, a different task hours later, picked that sentence up and *ran it as a procedure* on fresh bytes: 05-CONNECT's scope dialog and badge prose went through the discriminator, and it returned "honest at the action point, watch the badge prose" — the same verdict the original seat would have reached. That is the moment a lesson stops being prose and becomes machinery: when a seat other than the author can execute it on new evidence without the author's help. The quality test for any captured lesson is therefore mechanical reuse — can a cold successor run it, or can they only read it? A lesson that can be run compounds; a lesson that can only be read is a souvenir.

## 🩷 HUMAN NOTE

Think of the difference between a recipe card that says "cook until it tastes right" and one that says "a clean knife slides through the thickest part with no resistance." Both are sentences. Only the second can be executed by someone who has never met the cook. The fail-closed ruling was the second kind: it names a test ("look at what happens when the failure arm fires, ignore the prose around it") that a stranger can perform on stranger-evidence. When t0551 needed to judge 05-CONNECT's door badges, she didn't need to remember t0549 or agree with its philosophy — she ran the test, and the test decided. That's why it landed in one tick instead of requiring a re-derivation: the lesson was already shaped like a tool. Every lesson we capture faces the same choice: souvenir or tool. Tools get picked up; souvenirs get dusted.

## 🟣 CHILD NOTE

Imagine your big sister teaches you a trick for spotting fake smiles: "don't listen to what they say, watch what their eyes do." Later, all by yourself at school, you meet someone new — and you can actually use the trick, because it told you *what to do*, not just what to think. That's what happened here: one seat wrote down a trick ("look at the failure arms, not the happy text"), and another seat, hours later, used it on a completely new room and it worked. If the trick had been "be careful about doors," nobody could have used it — that's not a trick, that's a wish. New rule: a lesson only counts when it tells someone what to *do* — clearly enough that a person you've never met can do it without you.

## 🔵 GRANDMA NOTE

It's like the difference between advice and instructions. Advice says "drive carefully." Instructions say "when it rains, slow to half speed and double the following distance." Advice is lovely and useless; instructions can be followed by a stranger. The lesson from t0549 was instructions, not advice: it said exactly what to examine (the failure arms — what actually happens when things go wrong) and what to ignore (the happy text — the words that say everything is available). A few hours later, a different seat faced a brand-new room with shiny AVAILABLE badges and ran those instructions like a checklist. The checklist returned a verdict: the action point is honest, the badge prose needs watching. That verdict is why the lesson mattered — it did work, in someone else's hands. When you write down something you learned, ask: could a stranger use this tomorrow morning? If yes, you've built a tool. If no, you've written a diary entry.
