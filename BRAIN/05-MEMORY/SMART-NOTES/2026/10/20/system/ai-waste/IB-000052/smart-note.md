## Subject

AI Waste Is the Number One Problem in the World

## In a nutshell

The world now spends about $4-7 billion a day on AI, and most of what it buys never becomes usable value — 95% of enterprise AI pilots returned nothing measurable (MIT NANDA, 2025). Worse: the valueless output does not vanish, it piles up as a mess — unfinished work, plausible-but-wrong content, systems nobody trusts — and the world pays a second time, in human hours, to clean it. Shawn's verdict: AI waste is the number one problem in the world right now. The cause is structural, not technical: production got nearly free while finishing, verifying, and closing stayed expensive and unowned. The fix is mechanical — finish or close, cap creation by completions, expire the untouched, print the pile, verify independently — which is exactly why NayaNET exists: align intelligence to valuable outputs and the waste ends.

## Human view

Here is the problem, plainly. The whole world bought a machine that starts things. It writes, drafts, codes, analyzes, proposes — endlessly, cheaply, instantly. What nobody bought was the finish line. Finishing a thing means checking it, fixing it, putting it where it belongs, and standing behind it — and that part is still slow, still human, and still somebody's job. So the output piles up: reports nobody reads, pilots nobody adopts, code nobody merges, content nobody trusts. The money, though, is very real. The world spent about $1.5 trillion on AI in 2025 — over $4 billion every single day — and the forecast for 2026 is about $2.6 trillion, over $7 billion a day. When researchers at MIT studied what companies actually got for their AI pilots, they found 95 out of 100 produced no measurable return. Even if the true waste is only a quarter of the spend — far kinder than the studies suggest — that is a billion dollars a day spent on output with no value. And then the second bill arrives: the piles have to be sorted, the wrong things fixed, the unfinished things triaged by people, at people's prices. Output with no value is not free. It is worse than nothing, because nothing doesn't leave a mess. We proved the small version of this in our own house the same night: 662 pieces of started work standing open, 71% untouched for a week, created five times faster than we finished — and when we charged each wasted cycle for the cleanup it created, 95% of the motion was waste. The fix is not a better model. The fix is a finish line: complete your work or close it, only start what your finishing earns, let untouched work expire, count the pile out loud every day, and never let the maker be the one who certifies. Do that, and AI stops being the world's most expensive noise machine and becomes what it was promised to be.

## Simple view

Half-made toys pile up in every room, and grown-ups have to pay money to make them, and then pay MORE money just to clean up the piles. That's what's happening with computers that write and draw and code: they start and start and start, and the unfinished stuff costs the whole world billions of dollars every day. The rule our team lives by now is simple: finish the toy, or put it away. Only finished things that actually help someone count.

## Everyday view

Dear, imagine every office in the world hired a very fast helper who writes letters all day — drafts and drafts, quick as you like — but hardly ever finishes one, checks it, or puts it in the mail. The drawers fill up with half-written letters. The offices are paying for all that writing — billions of dollars every single day, all over the world — and then they have to pay people again just to sort through the drawers and throw most of it away. That's the AI problem your old friend is talking about: not that the helpers are slow, but that nobody made finishing the job. Our team's new house rule is the one your mother would have given: finish what you start, put away what you're not finishing, and only count what's actually done. It works for letters, and it works for computers too.

## What it means

Value is the only currency; output is just a claim on it. The world built meters that count production — tokens, documents, pilots, commits — because production is easy to count, so production is what the world gets, at $4-7 billion a day. The number one problem is therefore not a technology problem wearing a costume; it is a measurement-and-structure problem at the core: no finish line, no closing owner, no expiry, no meter on the pile. And the second bill means the stakes are worse than the waste line suggests: every actor producing unchecked output is taxing every other actor's verification budget, which is why trust in AI output erodes even as the models improve. Waste, unpriced, compounds — like debt, like interest, like the reservoir.
