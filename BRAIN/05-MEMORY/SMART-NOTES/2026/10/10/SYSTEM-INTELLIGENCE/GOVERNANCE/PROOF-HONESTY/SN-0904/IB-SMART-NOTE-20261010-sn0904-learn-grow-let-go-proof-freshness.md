## IN A NUTSHELL

On 2026-10-10 the source asked Shawn the freshness question: when we prove something today and then the code, policy, or environment changes — should the old proof still count? And the harder version: should proof expire just because time passed, even if nothing changed? Shawn answered with the sentence he said distills everything to its essence:

> "We learn and we grow and we let everything else go."

The law, as recorded:

1. **Proof never expires by time.** There is no clock on it. A proof taken a year ago against an unchanged world is as good as yesterday's.
2. **Proof expires the moment the world it was proven against changes.** Code, policy, environment, evidence-eligibility — when any material dependency moves, the proof no longer covers the new situation.
3. **The system always judges against the world as it is right now, never the world as it was.** Today's decisions run on today's world.
4. **The old proof stays in the history books, but it doesn't get a vote on today's decisions.** Preserved, labeled, retired from authority — never deleted, never voting.
5. **We keep what it taught us, and we let the certificate go.** The learning compounds; the certificate doesn't linger.

This resolves the D18 freshness question the source posed: "re-check when the world changes" is not just enough — it is the whole rule. No time-based expiry on top of it, because time alone changes nothing; and no holding on, because "at any moment, anything can change" and clinging to an outdated certificate is the opposite of intelligence.

It also answers Shawn's own earlier question ("how fresh proof has to be before it expires," 2026-10-10 ~12:01 PDT): freshness was never about age. A proof is fresh while its world is current.

Durable rule: **we learn and we grow and we let everything else go. No clock on proof; the world changing is the expiry. History keeps the record; today keeps the vote.**

**CLARIFICATION (Shawn, 2026-10-10 ~12:14 PDT) — what "let go" does and does not touch.** When he says distill everything to its essence — learn, grow, let everything else go — he is talking about **intelligence**, not code or interfaces. This doctrine must never be read as permission to strip code down, remove interfaces, or make anything less than what should be there. Code stays elite-level: everything the system needs, complete, no less. What gets "let go" is the outdated *understanding* — the old conclusion, the superseded certificate, the stale mental model. The overlap: when the world changes you don't keep the OLD interface out of nostalgia — you build the interface the new world needs, at full elite level. Letting go of the old form never means shrinking the capability. Distill = keep the essence, release the husk. For intelligence, the learning is the essence and the outdated claim is the husk. For code, the capability is the essence — capability is never released, only its outdated form, and only when replaced by what the current world needs. Everything is relevant; think about it intelligently and logically, and get the point.

## HUMAN NOTE

Think of a map. A map doesn't go bad because it's old — it goes bad because a road moved. You don't throw away last year's map on New Year's Day out of superstition; you replace the page when the highway gets rerouted. And you keep the old map in the atlas, because it tells you where you've been — you just don't navigate today's drive with it. That's the whole doctrine: no expiry dates, only changed roads. Learn the route, grow past it, let the old page go.

## CHILD NOTE

If you learned that the store is on Main Street, that stays true until the store moves. It doesn't become untrue just because a week passed. But once the store moves, you don't keep going to the old address — you learn the new one and let the old one go. That's how proof works: it lasts until the world changes, then you learn the new world.

## GRANDMA NOTE

It's the wisdom of not living in the past. What you proved yesterday stands — until the situation changes. When it changes, you don't pretend the old answer still fits, and you don't burn the old notebook either. You keep what it taught you, and you turn the page. Learn, grow, let go — that's not just good engineering, it's a good life.

## NAYA NOTE

Cold successor: never apply time-based expiry to proof, and never honor a proof whose material dependencies changed. For every qualification you inherit, check: is the world this was proven against still the world we're acting in — same code, same policy, same environment, same evidence-eligibility? If yes, the proof stands regardless of age. If anything material changed, the proof is historical: preserve it, label what changed, re-prove the changed part. Do not keep two ledgers (one "expired by age," one "expired by change") — there is one rule. And do not cling: "we learn and we grow and we let everything else go" means the system's memory is for learning, not for voting. The past teaches; only the present decides.
