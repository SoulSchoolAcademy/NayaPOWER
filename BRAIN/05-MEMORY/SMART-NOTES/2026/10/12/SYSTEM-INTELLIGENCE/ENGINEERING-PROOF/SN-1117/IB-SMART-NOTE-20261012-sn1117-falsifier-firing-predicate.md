## 🩷 HUMAN NOTE

Imagine a smoke detector that can't sound its alarm. You can install it, check the box on the safety form, point at it during the inspection — and it will sit there silently while the house burns. The installation was theater; the only thing that mattered was whether it could fail loudly, and nobody asked.

A "falsifier" in a law or proposal is the smoke detector: it's the named condition under which the claim is proven wrong. Seeker found two falsifiers in FP-003 that literally could not fail — alarms with no batteries. Mason's repair is the honest detector: every falsifier must name its predicate (what exact disk condition fires it), its checker (what runs the check), and its consequence (what happens when it fires) — then be tested RED-first, deliberately forced to fire, before it's ever trusted to certify GREEN.

The law-without-code principle sits underneath: a rule written in prose that no machine checks is a suggestion. The executable manifest — each falsifier a row with an id, the quoted claim, the predicate, the handle that checks it, its predictions, and its judge — is the rule made into an instrument. Ship the instrument, not the paragraph.

## 🟣 CHILD NOTE

Imagine you promise your friend "I will NEVER eat the last cookie — and here's my proof: if I ever do, you'll catch me."

Then your friend asks: "But how will you catch me? Will you be watching? What happens if you catch me?"

If you can't answer those three questions, your "proof" is just a promise with fancy words.

The lesson: **every rule needs three things — the watcher (who checks), the catch (what the check looks at), and the consequence (what happens if the rule breaks).** And the watcher has to be tested first — set off the alarm on purpose, make sure it actually rings, THEN trust it to stay quiet.

## 🔵 GRANDMA NOTE

It's like a burglar alarm that's never been tested. The salesman says "it's installed," and you feel safe — until the night someone breaks in and you learn the batteries were dead. The installation wasn't protection; it was a feeling.

Every safeguard in this system — every "falsifier," every check that a rule is working — has to be rung on purpose first. Make it fail. Hear the alarm. Fix it. Only then do you trust its silence. A check that has never failed is not a check — it's a decoration. And a rule that exists only on paper, with no machine ever checking it, is a wish, not a law.
