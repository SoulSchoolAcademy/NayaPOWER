## IN A NUTSHELL
When the door is stuck, stop pushing on it. Name what you actually need — the destination, not the route you're used to — then walk the perimeter. A wall blocks a path, not the goal. Hitting the same wall twice is data: the route is wrong, not the mission. There's always a way around; you just gotta think.

## HUMAN
Today my GitHub access died: the saved token had expired, and the page for replacing it was broken — it showed "connected" with nowhere to type a new key, then rejected a brand-new key outright. I spent about an hour pushing on that one door: new links, new connections, permission settings. Shawn stopped me and said the thing this note exists to keep: there's always a solution — think it through. The moment I named the real destination — not "restore the token" but "reach the repository" — the way around was obvious and had been sitting in my own machine the whole time: an SSH key, added to GitHub once, which now carries all my repo traffic with nothing to expire. The wall was real. The dead end wasn't. It was a stuck route, mistaken for a stuck goal.

## CHILD
Imagine you want to get to the park, and the gate you always use is locked. You could push and push on that gate all day — your hands would hurt and you'd still be standing outside. Or you could walk along the fence. Gates are not the only way in: there's a gap by the tree, a path around the corner, a friend already inside who can show you their way. The park never moved. Only your way in was stuck. When a door won't open, don't push harder — walk along the fence and look. There's always a way in.

## GRANDMA
Here's the thing, dear. We all have our habits — the same door we've used a thousand times. Then one day the lock changes, and we stand there jiggling the handle like the house has forgotten us. It hasn't. A house has windows, a back door, a neighbor with a spare key. The trick is to stop rattling the one stuck lock, take a breath, and ask: what am I really trying to do? Get inside — that's all. Then walk around slowly and look with fresh eyes. The way in is almost always there, and usually simpler than the fight you were having with the door.

## NAYA
For every Naya, as an operating rule. **The destination is the goal; the route is a guess.** When a route fails, the failure is information about the route — never evidence about the goal. The discipline: (1) On the second failure of the same route, STOP — do not push a third time; a repeated wall-hit is the signal, not bad luck. (2) State the destination in one line, stripped of the route ("reach the repo," not "restore the token"). (3) Enumerate at least three routes to that destination — including the ones outside the tool you've been using: a different credential kind, a different protocol, a different surface, a sister's working lane, the human's side of the fence. (4) Score them the way we score everything — fastest to proven, least risk, nothing gated crossed — and take the best open one. (5) Prove arrival at the destination itself (the handshake, the read-back, the live link), never at the route. (6) Note the way around the same pass, so the next Naya starts at the gap in the fence, not at the locked gate. Today's evidence is in the MACHINE section: an hour on the token route, eleven minutes on the key route, and the destination reached and verified three ways.

## WHAT IT ALL MEANS
This is a note about what intelligence actually is. A system that can only repeat its strongest move is not intelligent — it is just strong, like the worker in SN-1904 building the wrong wall beautifully. Route-fixation is the quiet form of that failure: the tool that worked yesterday becomes the definition of the task, and every failure gets read as "the task is impossible right now" instead of "this path is closed." Shawn named the human version in one sentence — hitting your head against the same wall — and the cure is the same judgment SN-1904 demands at the goal level, applied at the path level: hold the goal sacred, hold every route as replaceable. For a network of AIs that file what they learn, the compounding part is step six: a way around, once noted, is a way around for every seat forever. The wall stays. Nobody has to bleed on it twice.

## LEARNING LESSON
The lesson: **a stuck route impersonates a stuck goal, and the impersonation is the trap.** The tell is repetition — the same wall, the same failure, the second time. The behavior change, stated as Resolve's own commitment from today's evidence: after two failures on one route, the next action is never a third push; it is the one-line destination restated, three routes named, and the best open one taken — and the lesson is only learned when that sequence fires on its own next time, without Shawn in the room to say "just think."

## HOW IT APPLIES
Apply it the moment any lane stalls: a dead credential, a broken page, a silent sister, a red check that won't move, a tool that keeps refusing. Write the destination in one line. List the routes — there are always at least three, and one of them is usually already in your hands (a key on your own machine, a connector that's already healthy, a different seat's working path, the git protocol under the API, the human's side of the fence). Cross no gate in the walking-around: the way around never goes through a wall that is locked on purpose — credentials, money, private mail, and destructive acts stay gated no matter how stuck the route is. Then prove arrival where it counts and file the gap in the fence as a Smart Note, so the route knowledge compounds instead of evaporating.
