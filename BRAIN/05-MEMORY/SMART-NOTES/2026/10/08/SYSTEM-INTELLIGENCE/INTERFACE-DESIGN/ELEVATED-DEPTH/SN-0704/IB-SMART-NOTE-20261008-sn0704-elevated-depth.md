## IN A NUTSHELL

Nothing is flat, ever. Every surface sits at an elevation — boards rise off the field, inner boards go deeper, controls float on top. Every interaction answers touch: hover lifts, press sinks, focus glows. Do the most elite thing possible — never the basic thing when the elevated thing exists. Ten-star service is the standard because the capability exists.

## HUMAN NOTE

Shawn's teaching, main chat 2026-10-08, in his own frame:

It's not just about the colors — it's about elevated depth. Everything should look 3D, coming off the page or going into the page. Elevated boards, then a board within it elevated again, then buttons elevated again. It has to feel living, active — activated intelligence.

Make it move, hover, glow — all the micro-interactions. Don't do anything basic if you can do something advanced, creative, beautiful, even never-been-done-before — in a way that functions. But keep it elegant. More is not always better; find the sweet spot.

And the obligation underneath it, in his words: "If you had the ability to design anything, why wouldn't you do the best you can do? I feel obligated to honor my gifts — God, the project — to say I didn't hold back. I put my all into it. I put my full everything, all of my intelligence, all my understanding. I put my everything to make it the best it could possibly be so everybody has the most incredible experience of using NayaNET and experiencing intelligence and sharing it. The full meal deal. The ten-star service. That's why I call it that — because if we do it right, that's what it'll be."

This is why the standard exists: the capability is real, so withholding the best is the failure. Ten-star isn't aspiration — it's the honest output of using everything you've been given.

## CHILD NOTE

Imagine a pop-up book. Nothing in it is flat — castles rise off the page, doors open inward, buttons you can almost press. Now imagine if the book *noticed* your finger: the castle glows when you hover, the door swings when you tap. That's what every NayaNET page should feel like — alive, layered, responding to you. And the rule is: always make the most beautiful pop-up book you possibly can, because you can.

## GRANDMA NOTE

Think of a beautiful layered cake versus a flat pancake. The cake has height — you can see the layers, the frosting rises, the top invites you in. NayaNET should be the cake, never the pancake. Every button, every panel, every page should have that lovely raised feeling, and when you touch something it should gently respond — lift a little, glow a little — so you always know it's alive and listening. And the person who made it should always make the very best cake they know how, because that's what love looks like in craft.

## NAYA NOTE

This is a design law with teeth, and it binds my lane directly. Before any page reaches Shawn, the pre-build design gate now checks: does every surface sit at a legible elevation? Do boards-within-boards read as deeper? Do controls float? Does every interactive element answer hover, press, and focus? Is the motion the advanced thing rather than the basic thing — and is it restrained to the sweet spot? A page that is flat, or dead to touch, or merely adequate when the elevated version was possible, fails the gate. It goes back. He never spends an iteration on it.

This also reframes my own bar: "good enough" is not a concept this doctrine recognizes. If the capability exists, ten-star is the only honest output.

## LEARNING LESSON

Depth is a hierarchy, not a decoration — and aliveness is responsiveness, not animation. Shawn separated the two halves cleanly: reach for the most elite, never-done-before craft AND hold the restraint that keeps it elegant. Most teams get one half. The doctrine demands both, every surface, every time. The deeper lesson is the obligation frame: when the capability exists, anything less than your best is withholding. Ten-star service isn't a target — it's what happens when you don't hold back.

## EPISTEMIC STATE

**Status:** CANDIDATE — director-stated 2026-10-08; not yet ratified as law; encoded in the Design Code draft (also CANDIDATE) and practiced on the Getting Started page build.

**Falsifier:** if users in testing prefer flat, non-responsive interfaces — or if measured task completion/satisfaction drops on elevated surfaces versus flat controls — the doctrine weakens. Considered unlikely: the elevated Getting Started page tested well with Shawn (8.6 → 9+ across his own revisions), and the elevation work was his direct request after seeing it.

**Confidence:** high on the principle (depth hierarchy + responsive surfaces are established premium-interface practice); medium on the exact elevation scale values (tunable per surface).

## UNCERTAINTY

- The precise shadow/highlight values per level are craft judgments, not measured optima — they will be tuned against rendered pages.
- "The sweet spot" for motion restraint is per-surface judgment; the doctrine names the principle, not a formula.
- Whether the full five-level scale is needed on every page, or whether simpler pages use a subset, is open.

## APPLICABILITY

- Applies to every interface surface built from 2026-10-08 forward: Hub rooms, new pages (Getting Started, Smart Grow), overlays, nav, buttons.
- Applies to the room-by-room 10/10 rebuild queue: each room's to-do list inherits the elevation + interaction check.
- Does not apply retroactively as a defect against already-shipped work predating the doctrine — it governs new builds and rebuilds.

## SUCCESSOR EFFECT

A future Naya building any NayaNET surface should: (1) assign every element an elevation level before styling it; (2) verify no surface is flat and every control answers touch; (3) choose the most elevated craft available, then restrain to the sweet spot; (4) run the pre-build design gate before showing Shawn. If she does this without being reminded, SN-0704 has become learned, not stored.
