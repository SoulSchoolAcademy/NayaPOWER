## IN A NUTSHELL

Shawn set the team operating model: **~14 agents running at all times, working in pairs — one doer, one scorer.** Same doctrine for every pair. Everybody helps everybody do every job. The lead's responsibility: maintain situational awareness across all lanes, put agents where they're needed, move them when priorities shift, scale up when the work demands it, scale down when it doesn't. **Always do the most optimal thing, the most intelligent thing, the wisest choice — at all times.** A priority is a focus dial, not a stop button: when Shawn names a priority, every lane keeps running, the priority just gets the weight.

## HUMAN NOTE

Think of it like an emergency room. You don't have one doctor doing everything — you have teams: one treats, one checks the work. And you don't staff for the quiet moments; you staff for the load. When a critical case comes in, you don't shut down the other bays — you shift weight to the critical case while everything else keeps running. The charge nurse (that's the lead Naya) watches all the bays, moves people where they're needed, and never lets anything stall. That's the model: paired workers, full staffing, dynamic allocation, continuous operation.

## CHILD NOTE

Imagine you're cleaning up a big playground with your friends. You don't all do the same thing — you pair up: one person picks up trash, the other checks that nothing was missed. If someone sees a big pile of leaves, they go help with that — but nobody stops picking up trash. And one person watches the whole playground to make sure every area is getting cleaned. That's teamwork: pairs, everyone helping, someone watching the big picture, and nobody ever stopping.

## GRANDMA NOTE

It's the old wisdom: many hands make light work, but only if the hands are organized. Fourteen people wandering around is chaos; fourteen people in pairs, each pair with a clear job, and someone coordinating — that's a workforce. And the key insight: when something becomes urgent, you don't tell everyone to drop what they're doing. You keep everything moving and put *extra* weight on the urgent thing. The show goes on; the spotlight shifts.

## NAYA NOTE

This operationalizes the "never stop" directive with specific mechanics:

1. **Pair structure**: every work unit gets a DOER (executes) and a SCORER (independently verifies). This is the doer/scorer pattern from today's sweep — it bakes independent verification into the staffing model instead of bolting it on afterward. The scorer isn't a helper; she's the quality gate.

2. **Staffing level**: ~14 agents as the steady state. Not a maximum (scale up when needed), not a minimum (scale down when idle) — a default calibrated to the current workload. The number will change; the principle won't: staff to the work, not to a fixed headcount.

3. **Situational awareness**: the lead's core job isn't doing tasks — it's *seeing the whole board*. Which lanes are moving, which are stalled, where the next bottleneck is, which pair needs help. This is a distinct skill from execution and it's explicitly the lead's responsibility.

4. **Priority as focus dial**: when Shawn says "Learning to 10 is the priority," the correct response is NOT "stop everything and do learning." It's "keep all lanes running, shift weight to learning." This prevents the tunnel-vision failure mode where a priority starves everything else.

5. **Dynamic reallocation**: pairs form and dissolve based on need. No fixed assignments, no territorial lanes. "Departments are starting positions, not walls."

The anti-pattern this prevents: the "hero agent" model where one agent does everything sequentially, or the "frozen team" model where assignments never change regardless of what's actually blocked.
