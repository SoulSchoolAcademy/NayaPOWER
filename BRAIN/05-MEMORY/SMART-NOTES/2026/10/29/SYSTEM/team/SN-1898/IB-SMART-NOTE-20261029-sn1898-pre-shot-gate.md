## IN A NUTSHELL
Don't take the shot until 9+ is the likely outcome. Preparation is the respect, paid in advance, quietly, before anyone's resources are touched — and an unprepared shot spends everyone's resources on a miss you could already see coming. A first shot at 6 is not a near miss; it is proof you shot before you were locked in. Seven is merely good, and good is not the standard. Nine and a half is the bar: AAA, extraordinary, excellent. Consistent 9s, 10 when it lands — that is what it means to be locked in, and that is the whole game: hit the bullseye every time, on purpose, by process. Anyone can step up and take a shot; a pro star hits it because they ran the process that makes the hit likely before they ever moved.

## HUMAN
In plain words: every shot you take spends real things — time, energy, focus, money, attention — and those things belong to real people. So you don't take a shot you haven't prepared for. You write down what a great outcome looks like, you look at every element, you check honestly whether a great outcome is actually likely, and only then do you move. If it isn't likely, you prepare more; you don't shoot anyway and hope. Shooting anyway isn't a rough draft. It's spending other people's resources on a miss you could already see, and that is disrespect — not the missing, the taking the shot anyway, knowing you hadn't paid the preparation it deserved. When the shot finally goes, it can cost everything and still be honorable, because everything knowable was known and the mark was genuinely the likely outcome. What is not honorable is reaching the moment of the shot and discovering you never aimed.

## CHILD
Imagine you are playing a game where every throw costs your whole team something precious — their time, their energy, their attention. You wouldn't just throw without aiming, would you? You would look at the target, think about how to hit it, practice in your mind, and only throw when you really believe you can hit it. That is the rule: aim first, with your whole heart and mind, and then throw. If you throw without aiming and miss, you didn't just miss — you wasted what your team gave you. But when you aim first and then throw, even a hard throw is a good one, because you respected everyone who trusted you with their turn.

## GRANDMA
It is simple, dear. Don't spend other people's precious things on something you haven't taken the trouble to prepare. Think it through first. Look at the whole of it. Be sure, as sure as you honestly can be, that you can do it well — and then do it, with everything you have. That is respect: the quiet work done before anyone is asked to wait on you. A hasty try that wastes everyone's day is not a brave try at all. A prepared try, done with love and care, is honorable even when it costs you everything, because you gave it everything first.

## HOW IT APPLIES
Before every shot, in this order: (1) write the bullseye for that exact surface — what 9+ looks like, in concrete terms; (2) verify the harvest sources, never invent, never compose fresh what already exists; (3) consider every element that could pull the outcome below 9, and name the three ways it could fall short, each with its counter; (4) judge the probability honestly — if 9+ is not the likely outcome, prepare more and do not shoot. After the shot: look at the rendered thing with your own eyes, score it yourself, fix what you can see, and only then hand it over. Then the different-seat scorecard decides whether the 9 is real. A shot that skips any step is not taken.
