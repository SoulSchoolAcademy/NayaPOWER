# Place the Literal Thing in Its Spot

SN-4357 · DECISION / FRAME

**Created:** 2026-11-20 23:42Z

Kinds: Honor · Multiplication · Craft · Knowledge

## Hook

Frame the story like the calendar.

## Nutshell

Whatever sits in the showcase — video, image, or text — sits inside a lit-up intelligent board inside the frame. Never a bare picture, video, or text on the page. The board is alive: light flows around its edge, it moves and breathes, and on touch it presses in and out and lights up — exactly like the Hub's intelligent boards. Text becomes elevated boards in that same area — three, two, or one, however the words display best. The player stays a real, pro player: bar at the top of the frame, the logo and a button where the playlist was. And the module does double work: the framed showcase IS a post — a framed image, video, or text in the feed shows up as a smart block, with only the social sharing block added underneath. For this presentation the social block is NOT added — but it is the same module, built once, used twice.

## Human

The frame is the unit, and the board is its inside. Everything he has corrected about this presentation has been one discipline in different clothes: take the frame from the calendar (SN-4283), pull the buttons real (SN-4286), put the literal player in (SN-4285) — and now, put a living board inside the frame. A showcase area with a picture pasted on it is a poster; a showcase area holding an intelligent board is a Naya object. The board does not need to go anywhere to earn its place — "even if it goes nowhere, it must still feel like a living board" — because aliveness is the feel of the thing, not its destination. And the double use is the same discipline at system scale: a post in NayaNET is a framed thing with sharing underneath, and a presentation slide is a framed thing without it — so the module built for the presentation IS the module that becomes the post. Build once, use twice, and the system gets simpler while the presentation gets richer. Two builds for the same frame would be the old mistake in a new shape.

## Child

Never describe the image. Every slide, over and over: this is what it is · this is why it matters · this is what's in it for you. Clear, direct, warm and inviting, not long. By the end, a child should understand it.

## Grandma

The presentation is the agreement's front door, and a front door blocked by its own furniture does not open. The person should meet the image, the video, or the text — full, clear, world class — and nothing else should compete with it.

## Naya

The presentation IS the story. All morning he has been building toward this: the 10-board distillation, the live walk to 27, and now the way it is shown — because a story no one can sit through is a story no one receives. His spec answers the only question that matters about a presentation: what does the person see, in what order, and what do they do next. They see the image first, big. They read a little. They click. The next slide tells them all about it. The sides switch, so the pattern never goes numb. The first slide is the door and the last slide is the deed — "View Agreement" at the start, AGREE and your name at the end — so the whole thing is one walk from the outside to the inside, and nothing in the middle asks them to guess what to do.

## What it means

1. The presentation IS the story — a story no one can sit through is a story no one receives.
2. The frame is the point of the fix — the agreement's storybook only feels like the calendar if it IS the calendar's frame, the real ornate silver with the crest set into the rails, not a drawing of it.
3. A link is not the thing — a link on the cover says "the player is over there"; the player in the frame says "here it is, press it."
4. A button is a real object, not a label with a border — depth, light, an icon in it — and his eye tells the difference in a glance.
5. The frame is the unit, and the board is its inside — a showcase area holding an intelligent board is a Naya object, and the module built for the presentation IS the module that becomes the post.

## Why it matters

1. A perfect presentation is not decoration on the agreement — it is how the agreement is received: nobody gets lost, nobody gets bored, nobody wonders what to click.
2. Every round that builds another hub costs the system its unity, and unity is the product — the Hub IS the Personal page, one page, one center, one front door.
3. Chrome is judged by what it hides, not by what it holds — a bar across the bottom turns a picture storybook into a page with a picture peeking out from behind a shelf.
4. His keeper lines are the promise, in his own words — the private room, the treasure chest, the neighborhood, the machine serving you — losing them would lose the promise.

## What changes now

1. When he hands over a player, a video, a button, a frame — place the literal thing in its spot. Never place a link to it on the cover, never a placeholder, never a picture of it.
2. Rebuild the storybook inside the calendar's own code: pull the calendar's HTML, keep the same frame and the same everything, reformat only the middle area for the slides, change the bar a little for the agreement's headliner.
3. When a build needs buttons, pull the button code from a page that has the real ones — the Hub's Collective / Personal / Activity buttons, or the Academy's originals. Never invent a flat substitute.
4. Build presentations with no bars: a slim top that folds away on scroll, a small Keep Reading button and a little back button at the bottom, calendar day forward/back logic, no slide count anywhere, name only at the end with the agreement.
5. When anything goes in the showcase area, it goes inside a lit-up intelligent board — video, image, or text, three boards or two or one, however the words display best.
6. Write every slide of the agreement in the formula, in the captain's hand: what it is · why it matters · what's in it for you — clear, direct, warm and inviting, not long, never a description of the image.
