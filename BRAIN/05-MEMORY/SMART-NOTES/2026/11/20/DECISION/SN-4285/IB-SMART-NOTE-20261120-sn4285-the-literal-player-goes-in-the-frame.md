## The Lesson

A link is not the thing. A link on the cover says "the player is over there"; the player in the frame says "here it is, press it." This is the same correction he has now given three times in three clothes: the frame was redrawn instead of taken from the calendar (SN-4283), the buttons were invented flat instead of pulled real (SN-4286), and the player was about to be linked instead of placed. The discipline is one discipline — harvest the real thing and put it where it belongs. A presentation that opens with the player already there, big, in the frame, starts the story right there; a presentation that opens with a link to the player starts with a detour. The cover invites. The frame holds the thing itself.

## Why It Matters

The first slide is the welcome, and the welcome is the video — Queen Naya speaking, the anthem playing. If the video lives behind a link on the cover, the first thing the person meets is a chore: click out, find it, come back. If the player is in the frame, the first thing they meet is her, speaking, in the frame he designed for exactly that. The top bar carries the balance — her picture on one side, the logo on the other — so the eye lands on the player, not on chrome. Every layer of the presentation exists to get out of the way of the story; the player in the frame is the story, already playing.

## How to Use It

When he hands over a player, a video, a button, a frame — place the literal thing in its spot. Never place a link to it on the cover, never a placeholder, never a picture of it. Score every opening screen by this: is the thing itself there, or only a pointer to it? A pointer goes back. And keep the top bar slim: her picture on one side, the logo on the other, balanced, so the story starts right there.
