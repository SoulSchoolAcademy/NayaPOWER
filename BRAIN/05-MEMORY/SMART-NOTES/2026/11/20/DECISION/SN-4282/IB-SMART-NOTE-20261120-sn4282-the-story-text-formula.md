## In a Nutshell

Never describe the image. Every slide, over and over: this is what it is · this is why it matters · this is what's in it for you. Clear, direct, warm and inviting, not long. The captain writes it herself, intentionally, matched to its image — agents are never left to put whatever in there. If the text won't fit beautifully with its image, it runs onto the next slide on Keep Reading; a text-only slide is proper, two in a row are okay. And by the end, a child should understand it.

## The Lesson

The formula replaces the description. The Queen's Story approach — writing the queen's words FROM what each image shows — was close, but it still let the words describe the picture. His correction cuts that out entirely: the words never say what the image looks like; they say what the thing IS, why it MATTERS, and what's IN IT FOR YOU. That is the whole slide, every slide, in the same shape, so the person never has to learn a new shape — they only have to listen. And the shape is the reason the story lands: three short answers, in order, in a warm voice, are something a child can follow all the way to the agreement, while a paragraph describing a picture is something a child skips.

Three rulings hold the formula up. The hook is his: "Imagine a new internet — one that's not a mess... 99% of people fail online. Why? Because it's a mess... What if — imagine an AI operated internet that organized your life and it was automatic, so the 99% of you that fail now, 100% you can have success." Keep it as "now you CAN" — the queen opening the door, not a guarantee of results. He approved it — "That's much better" — and it supersedes the captain's earlier hook. The voice is epic and real: "Yeah, that was epic. You can say that exactly… keep going with that kind of passion and realness" — the passion and realness stay, the description-heavy form does not. And the writer is named: the captain writes the official text intentionally, because text written by whoever happens to be building the slide is text with no voice, and the agreement's voice is the whole point. The overflow rule is the same discipline on the layout side: never squeeze the words to fit the frame — let the story run onto the next slide, beautifully, one image at a time.

## Why It Matters

A picture with a description beside it is a normal webpage. A picture whose own short story is told in her voice is the kingdom — and the formula is what makes the telling repeatable across 27 slides without going flat or going generic. The child standard is the test: "By the end it's a child should understand it." If a child can follow it to the end, anyone can; if a child can't, the words are still describing instead of telling. And the read-aloud delivery — "Keep speaking as they come" — is how he checks it: slide by slide, in his ear, in real time, so where the voice isn't hers, he hears it at once. He liked it: "Yeah, quite like it."

## How to Use It

Write every slide of the agreement in the formula, in the captain's hand: what it is · why it matters · what's in it for you — clear, direct, warm and inviting, not long, never a description of the image. Slide 1 opens with his approved hook, in the epic voice he approved. If the text won't fit beautifully with its image, let it run onto the next slide on Keep Reading; text-only slides are proper, two in a row are okay. Read it aloud to him slide by slide as it's written, and keep the voice a child can understand all the way to the end.
