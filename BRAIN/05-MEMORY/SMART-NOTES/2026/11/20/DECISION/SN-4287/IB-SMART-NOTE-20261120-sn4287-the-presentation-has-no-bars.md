## The Lesson

Chrome is judged by what it hides, not by what it holds. A bottom bar that carries the navigation but covers the slides has failed at its one job — the person came to see the slides, and the bar is in the way. So the bar goes, not the navigation: Keep Reading and a small back button, sitting low and small, do the moving, and they look like part of the presentation because they are part of it, not furniture around it. The same subtraction runs through the rest of the rulings: the headline folds away instead of pinning itself over the picture; the slide count disappears instead of stamping a number on every frame; the name waits until the end instead of stopping the person at the door. Each removal gives the picture more room, and the picture is the point. And the navigation is not invented — the calendar already solved forward and back with its day buttons, so the presentation borrows that logic the same way it borrows the calendar's frame (SN-4283) and the Hub's buttons (SN-4286). Harvest, never reinvent, and subtract whatever blocks the view.

## Why It Matters

The presentation is the agreement's front door, and a front door blocked by its own furniture does not open. The person should meet the image, the video, or the text — full, clear, world class — and nothing else should compete with it. A slide count turns a story into a progress bar; a name at the door turns a welcome into a form; a bar across the bottom turns a picture storybook into a page with a picture peeking out from behind a shelf. Removing them is not minimalism for its own sake — it is the story getting the whole frame, the way he designed it: the image fits between the bar and the frame, as big as possible, alive and moving, and the frame stays static around it. The captain's viewpoint walk is the guard on all of it: every viewpoint, every frame, extraordinary, nothing blocked, premium, readable, world class — or it does not reach him.
