## Human

The lesson is about the reader's body, not the writer's words. A long board is not hard to read because the words are hard — it is hard to read because the eye has nowhere to rest and the thumb has nothing to do. "Unfold, unfold, next" is a rhythm for the hand as much as for the mind: each click is a small completion, each board a small promise kept, and the reader keeps going because going is easy. A wall of text reverses that: the reader must decide, mid-scroll, whether the rest is worth it — and most people will not decide, they will leave. That is why he says it twice in two ways: "if they skip it they're skipping valuable information" is the cost, and "make it readable" is the fix. The two smaller orders ride the same insight: two buttons that do the same job force a choice that should not exist (keep reading IS what's next — one button), and gray text is a whisper where white text is a voice — if the words matter, say them in the colour people can read. The fix round's failure mode is the proof of the law's teeth: counting paragraphs instead of sentences let a six-sentence board through, because the maker measured the container, not the reading. The law counts sentences because the reader reads sentences.

## Child

Imagine reading a really long story with no pictures and no pages — just one giant wall of words! You'd get tired and stop, right? But if the story comes in little cards, three little sentences on each card, and you tap "keep reading" to get the next card — that's fun! Tap, tap, tap, and suddenly you've read the whole story without even noticing!

## Grandma

It's like serving a big dinner, love — you don't pile everything on one plate! You serve it in lovely little courses, one after another, so it's a pleasure to eat. Three little sentences on a beautiful board, then the next one, then the next — unfold, unfold, next. And only one button to press, not two doing the same job. And the words are always white on black, clear as day — never that faint grey you have to squint at.

## Naya

I break the reading into little boards — three sentences, then the next — so nobody has to climb a wall of words to reach something valuable. One button, white words, unfold, unfold, next.

## What it means

- **THE COUNT:** every ~3 sentences a new elevated board; max 4 sentences per board. The 4-board / 9-board numbers are his sense of scale, not a quota.
- **THE RHYTHM:** unfold — one board per Keep-reading click. The fix is the length, not the rhythm.
- **THE BUTTON:** one button only ("Keep reading"). "What's next" goes — it is the same job, and two buttons for one job force a choice that should not exist.
- **THE COLOUR:** white text, always. Gray text goes — he hates gray text.
- **THE MEASURE:** sentences, counted sentence-by-sentence on the served bytes — never paragraphs, never `<p>` counts. A six-sentence board inside three paragraphs still fails.

## Why it matters

Readability is not politeness, it is retention: the agreement's value lives in its later steps, and a reader who stops at step two never meets steps three to ten. Every board that is too long is a door the reader does not walk through, and the information behind it is lost — not because it was wrong, but because it was hard to reach. The law makes reading cheap: three sentences is a breath, a click is a step, and a whole agreement becomes a walk instead of a climb. That is what "experts at making it easy to take in text" means — not shorter words, but smaller boards.

## What changes now

1. No board carries more than 4 sentences; the target is ~3. Counted sentence-by-sentence, on the served bytes.
2. One button only — "Keep reading." No second button doing the same job.
3. White text throughout; no gray text anywhere it can be helped.
4. The rhythm stays unfold: one board per click, in order, to the end.
5. A checker that counts paragraphs instead of sentences has not checked the law.
