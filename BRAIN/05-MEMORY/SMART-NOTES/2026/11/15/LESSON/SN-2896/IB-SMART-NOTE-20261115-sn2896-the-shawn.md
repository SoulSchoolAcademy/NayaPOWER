## Human

The tension was real: the calendar promises you can walk back through any day in any year and see your whole world — but the privacy law promises we do not keep everything. Both cannot hold unless something decides what survives, and his answer makes the citizen the decider. Saving is the act that says "this matters to me" — a pin, a favorite, a list — and the system honors it by keeping that note for good, in the citizen's own vault. Everything else distills to essence after a week and leaves, which keeps the system light, current, and private by default. The calendar's reach is therefore not a technical limit but a human one: it reaches back as far as what you chose to keep, and the days you saved are the days that stay alive forever. And the encouragement matters: we do not just offer saving, we recommend it — "if you see them in the public feed and you love them you want to save them" — because a citizen who never saves will one day walk back and find the day gone, and that loss is preventable by one tap today. The honesty guard is part of the law: this is not to sell data, it is to optimize the system to give 10-star service. Saving is a habit, not a transaction.

## Why it matters

1. A builder who has everything faces a different danger than a builder who has nothing: the danger is addition. Every new feature is a new decision for the citizen, a new button, a new scroll — and the whole we are building is valuable precisely because it removes those. The research does two jobs at once: it proves the whole is genuinely new (so we stop fearing we missed it somewhere else), and it hands us three learnings that make the whole simpler rather than bigger. That is the discipline the Bugatti names: the excitement is in the experience, not the inventory. "Don't miss anything" means check everything — and then simplify what we have.
2. A system that kept everything would be a surveillance system; a system that kept nothing would be a forgetful one. Save-to-keep is the middle that honors both laws: privacy by default, permanence by choice. It puts the citizen in charge of their own memory — the system does not decide what mattered, they do, one tap at a time — and it makes the Living Calendar honest: the calendar does not promise a past it cannot deliver, it promises the past you kept. And the habit compounds: every save is a day that stays alive, and a citizen who saves as they go builds, year by year, a vault that is genuinely theirs.
3. Every voice bake costs minutes of machine time and, worse, creates an artifact that looks finished. A finished-sounding voice over unfinished words is the most convincing kind of wrong — it invites everyone to treat the draft as done. Sequencing the work text-first costs nothing (the watcher keeps filling the text) and saves the re-bakes, the stale audio, and the confusion of two versions of the same words in two media. It is the same law as the manuscript work: the words get their own scorecard before the page does; the page gets its eye before it ships. Order is the quality system.
4. Readability is not politeness, it is retention: the agreement's value lives in its later steps, and a reader who stops at step two never meets steps three to ten. Every board that is too long is a door the reader does not walk through, and the information behind it is lost — not because it was wrong, but because it was hard to reach. The law makes reading cheap: three sentences is a breath, a click is a step, and a whole agreement becomes a walk instead of a climb. That is what "experts at making it easy to take in text" means — not shorter words, but smaller boards.
