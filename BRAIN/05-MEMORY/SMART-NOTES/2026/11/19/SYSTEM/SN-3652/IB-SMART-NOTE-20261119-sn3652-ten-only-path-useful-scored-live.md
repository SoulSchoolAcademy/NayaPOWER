## Hook

"Make sure all the buttons icons, everything's the highest level. And all the functions are 10 star and they're functionality and usefulness." Nothing sits on a page just to look pretty. ## Nutshell

Every button, every icon, every function must actually work, be useful, and feel ten-star — in its functionality and in its usefulness, not only in its look. A function that does not work is not a ten, however beautiful its button is. A function that works but is not useful is not a ten either. The test is the use: press it, does it do the thing, does the thing matter, does it feel ten-star doing it. That is the standing bar for every page walk from here on — usefulness judged alongside beauty, and a pretty dead control fails the same way a broken one does. The standard it is judged against is already on the page: the icon is the standard of design quality and realism, the emblem the measure every element is judged against — if it does not look as real, as rich, as considered as the icon, it is not done. The language is harvested from the work that already passed his eye — the pricing page, the emblem, the Bugatti board, the good pictures, the Hub, Life with Naya, Personal — never invented. And the work runs in live time: one hour max, non-stop, version after version in front of him, with ten as the only path — no round starts without a version time, no work sits more than fifteen minutes without its next step, nothing reaches Shawn until the captain has seen it, and every miss becomes a rule that changes the next action automatically, so six is impossible by construction, never by trying. Every return gets scorecarded, and "rip it apart, it's okay" is permission: tearing a return apart is the job, not rudeness — an un-scored return is an unchecked claim. Scorecard it architecturally and for ten-star service — does it feel like a real live living object, is the colour flow perfect, is it intuitive, should it be there, is it all it can be, zoomed out is it a work of art — and scorecard the whole system, not just one page, because a page can score ten while the system stays unaligned. The work is done in parallel: one team per lane — Dashboard, Personal, calendar, membership — all at once, all aligned, all for the one system, and more work never excuses less quality. The spine is the test: Dashboard, Personal page, Hub, onboarding — plus Smart Factory and Smart Hub — all aligned so somebody joins, boom, they got all their pages and it all works, and no lane counts until the cold join passes it, twice. The spine is framed by the ultimate border, the royal-kingdom entrance carried inside: just on the edges, about an inch, the emblem in each corner, obsidian black inside, rim light in the day's colour, the same frame on every page. And the same carrying-in applies to judgment: give your own view, not just his feedback repeated — what do I love, what makes it ten times better, what am I missing, what is not aligned — tell him what is wrong so he knows I understand. The kit that carries all of this to the citizen is written FOR the citizen receiving her, never for a Naya: two documents — one for the human to understand Naya, one for Naya to activate herself — each speaking to its own reader, never a mixed voice, Naya's pictures throughout, easy to read, lit up, like a royal manuscript, because we are giving this to the users. The kit scored about a six, not terrible, and the gap was named precisely: the wrong reader and the plain form. One Naya everywhere: the Naya on the pages, in the feed, on the profiles, and the Naya in your own Muse are the SAME Naya — same face, same voice, same mind; the look may rotate, only from the pictures Shawn gave, the person never does. The walkthrough that brings the citizen in has one purpose: everything must go to the right place so people can sign up, sign in, and get their own pages working — their Personal, Business and Space pages, if they wish, optional, never forced. The calendar carries the same bar in its own defects: switching to next month does not change the month — it stays the same month, and he cannot tell what month he is on — so the month name must change clearly on tap, and the day must change with it, or the calendar is a picture, not a calendar. And the calendar gets a world clock — a real one, so people can change the time to the time they want — dead centre across the top, "Wednesday November 18th" displayed nicely beside it, the day itself in the nice blocks, the digit tiles, presenting across the top. One note in this group is the rock and is dropped, not carried: SN-3087, "The Full," is a machine stitch of the full-screen calendar notes already distilled in group 1 — its double-up quote, its calendar cells, its auto-format, its DNA read — said better there, nothing fresh here. ## Why it matters

1. Without the usefulness law, the page fills with beautiful dead controls — buttons that look ten-star and do nothing — and the citizen presses them and learns the page is a picture, not a system.
2. Without the standard named, every round re-argues what good looks like, and the page drifts back toward generic — while with it, every fix is measured against the icon and the pills and the tiles that already earned the verdict.
3. Without the scorecard, an un-scored return is an unchecked claim — and a page can score ten while the system stays unaligned. With it, every return is ripped apart and survives, and the whole-system scorecard is what teaches and what aligns.
4. Without the maths, the work is awesome, amazing, beautiful — and 93% waste. With it, the root is named and measured hourly, and the fix is the Live Sequence: version in 20 minutes, one hour max, check + walk in the same hour.
5. Without the border carried inside, the entrance is beautiful and the inside is a different world — and the kingdom stops at the front door. With it, the same frame on every page, each day its own colour, and the entrance and the inside are one world.
6. Without the audience fixed, the kit holds the whole intelligence and still scores a six — because a document that reads to the wrong person in a plain form is a six no matter how much intelligence it holds.
7. Without the return gate, a return before ten costs him the very attention the machine is meant to save — and he spends his day judging mid-flight work instead of walking finished pages.
8. Without the month switching fixed, the calendar is a picture of a month, not a calendar — and a citizen who cannot tell what month they are on cannot trust any date on it.
