## Human

"Looks cool" is the weakest possible reason to keep a picture, and the fastest way to fill a page with decoration that explains nothing. The test is one glance: can a stranger tell exactly what it is saying? The key says vault. Do-no-harm says the code. Old-vs-new internet says the break. Math and chain fail the test — they are abstractions that need their own words to be understood, which means the words are doing the work and the picture is just standing there. Holding them is not a loss; the files are kept, the words carry the scenes, and the door stays open for a vision that passes the test. The completeness audit is the other half of the same law: a page must say everything that matters AND nothing it cannot say clearly. Completeness without legibility is noise; legibility without completeness is a beautiful empty page.

## Child

Imagine you draw a picture to explain your favourite game. If your friend looks at it and says "that's cool!" but has no idea what game it is — the picture didn't do its job! The good pictures are the ones where your friend looks once and says "OH! I get it!" — a key for a safe place, a big sign that says DO NO HARM. If a picture needs a whole story just to explain the picture, we put it aside and let the words do the talking.

## Grandma

It's like this, love — a picture in a storybook has to tell the story all by itself. The key tells you "this is safe." The promise tells you "we do no harm." But a picture of numbers and little cubes? Lovely to look at, dear, but nobody knows what it means without you standing there explaining it. So we set those two aside — kept safe, not thrown away — and let the words carry those pages. And we checked the whole story, front to back, to make sure nothing important was left out. That's completeness, love: everything that matters, nothing you can't understand.
