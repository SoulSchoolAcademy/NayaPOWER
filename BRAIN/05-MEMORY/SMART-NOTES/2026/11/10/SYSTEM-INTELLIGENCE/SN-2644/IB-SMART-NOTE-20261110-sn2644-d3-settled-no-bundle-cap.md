# SN-2644 — D3 Settled: No Bundle Cap — Mail Is a Choice at the Moment of Writing

## Hook
Ten friends ringing ten times in a minute is noise. One ring that says "ten friends are here" is the news. He asked for the explanation — then he thought it through and landed somewhere better: no cap at all. Mail is a choice at the moment of writing the note.

## Nutshell
Shawn asked at 02:05Z on 2026-11-10 because he did NOT understand the bundle cap — so he could not answer D3. Here is the explanation he was given, in plain words. The cap is a limit on how many "right now" mails one person gets in one hour for one project. Why it exists — flood protection: without it, ten big things in one hour means ten separate mails in that hour, and people stop reading them — a notification nobody reads is worse than no notification. How it works: the cap BUNDLES, it never drops. Over the cap, the extra news waits and rides along in the next mail (or the digest) — nothing important is lost, it just waits its turn so you actually read it. The child-simple version: like a doorbell — ten friends ringing ten times in a minute is noise; one ring that says "ten friends are here" is the news. The default was 1 per project per hour per person: if two major things happen in the same hour for the same project, the second waits and bundles. Tonight as the example: SN-2625 through SN-2629 landed in about 40 minutes — without a cap that would be 5 immediate mails to everybody; with the cap it is 1 bundled mail plus the digest. The captain's recommendation, answering his question "what would you do if it was your baby": KEEP 1 — immediate mails should be rare (only rubric +7 and above), so the cap almost never bites, and when it does, bundling is the kind thing to do; the cap is per project, so two projects can each send one in the same hour. Then at 02:12Z he settled D3 himself — and the settlement SUPERSEDES the spec default of 1/hour AND his own first thought of "three per hour" in the same message, because he thought it through and landed here. The answer is NOT limiting how many are sent per hour. The flaw he spotted in a count cap: a backlog makes the important thing wait behind unimportant things — if he sends 10 messages in two hours and the 10th is the really important one, it waits for hours behind the other nine. The design instead: every note lands as a notification IN the system — you log in, you see all your notifications, you click one, it opens the smart note. Mail is a CHOICE at the moment of writing the note: when you enter the note you choose "mail" or "no mail," and if mail, you attach the list or the person you want it to go to. The rule for choosing: if you're just saving the note for yourself, for the project, for a page, it's saved there — like a post, no mail. If you're posting it because you want your team to be aware of it, then you probably want to mail them. Smart notes → mail every time (they're official, shared by definition — that's why you write one). Naya Notes → probably not by default — mail only when you're writing it because you want it to reach the other person or team. Being mindful about what's necessary to communicate is part of being intelligent — the judgment lives at the source, the person writing the note, not in a queue at the end. Status: the rubric seat was notified the same pass; the notification spec's §8 D3 is amended with this ruling, and the cap is gone.

## Why it matters
A notification system that floods people trains them to ignore it — and a notification system people ignore is worse than no notification system at all, because it teaches the hive that the bell means nothing. The bundle cap was the first answer to that: bundle instead of dropping, so nothing important is ever lost, and wait instead of shouting, so the citizen actually reads it. The doorbell is the whole design in one image: one ring that says ten friends are here is the news; ten rings is noise. But he saw further, and the settlement is wiser than the cap: a count cap has a flaw no count can fix — a backlog makes the important thing wait behind unimportant things. The tenth message, the really important one, should not wait for hours behind the other nine. So the judgment moves to where it belongs: the source. The person writing the note decides, at the moment of writing, whether it mails and who it goes to — and every note is always there as a notification in the system, waiting to be opened. Being mindful about what's necessary to communicate is part of being intelligent. That is the deepest line in the ruling: intelligence is not a queue at the end, it is a choice at the beginning.
