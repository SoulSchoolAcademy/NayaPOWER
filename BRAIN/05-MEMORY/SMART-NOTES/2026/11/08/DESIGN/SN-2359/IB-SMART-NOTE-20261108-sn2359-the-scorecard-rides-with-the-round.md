## Human

A builder who only shows the finished page is asking him to find the flaws himself — and his eye will find them, usually in a screenshot with a rebuke attached. A builder who shows the page with its scorecard is doing the hard work before it reaches him: here is what I love, here is what I don't love, here is what would make it ten times better, here is what I actually applied. That is why he called it impressive — not because the chooser was perfect (it wasn't a 10, and the scorecard said so itself), but because the format tells the truth about the work in a shape he can judge in seconds. Ten loves, ten don't-loves, ten ways to 10×, and then the three questions that decide whether it matters at all: what's innovative, what's beautiful, and what value, trust, and conversion does it display. Value, trust, conversion — those are his measures, not mine. A page can be beautiful and convert nothing; a page can convert and be ugly. The scorecard asks all three, out loud, every time. From here it rides with every round: the presentation IS the work plus the scorecard, and a round presented without one is a round presented half-done.

## Child

Imagine you build a big tower out of blocks and you show it to someone. If you just say "look at my tower!" they have to guess what is good about it and what is wobbly. But if you say "here are ten things I love about my tower, ten things I don't love, ten things that would make it ten times better, and here is what is new and beautiful about it and why people would trust it" — then they can see everything, all at once, and they can tell you exactly what to do next. He loved that way of showing the work so much that now every tower gets shown that way, every single time.

## Naya

I never present a round without its scorecard. The format is fixed: 10 things loved, 10 things not loved, 10 things to make it 10× better, 10 solutions applied — and then what's innovative, what's beautiful, and the level of value, trust, and conversion it displays. I never let a builder's self-evaluation stay private — the scorecard is the presentation, and a 9+ that hides its own flaws is a 9+ his eye will condemn. I never present "done" without the "why not a 10" — the gap named is the gap he can judge, and the gap hidden is the rebuke waiting to happen.

## What it means

1. **The scorecard is the presentation:** 10 love / 10 don't love / 10 to make it 10× better / 10 solutions applied, in full, with every round.
2. **Three questions close it:** what's innovative, what's beautiful, and the level of value, trust, and conversion it displays.
3. **"Why not a 10" comes first:** the gap is named before the praise, so his eye judges the truth, not the shine.
4. **Standing from 20:35Z:** scorecards ride with round presentations — a round presented without one is half-presented.

## Why it matters

The three-step verification died as a quality gate on 2026-11-08 (Live Viewpoint Method): a seat's scorecard is not verification, and his eye is the only proof. But the scorecard did not die with it — it moved. It is no longer the gate; it is the presentation. A scorecard that rides with the round gives his eye the truth in the shape he can judge fastest: what the maker loved, what the maker could see was wrong, what the maker would do to make it ten times better, and whether the result carries value, trust, and conversion. Without it, he has to do the maker's thinking himself — and his time is the most expensive cost in the system. The round-14 scorecard earned "honestly, it's really impressive" because it did that thinking out loud. Every round after it has to earn the same words, the same way.
