## Nutshell

You build an app, a website, or a product — starting from what it does for one real person, shipping the smallest working version first, then growing it by what real users actually do with it.

## Human

Think of building a treehouse. You don't start with the roof — you start with the platform, one board nailed solid, somewhere a kid can stand. Then a wall, then a ladder, then the roof. Each piece is small enough to finish in a day, and the treehouse is usable long before it's finished. This project keeps the boards in order: what it does, the smallest working version, and what real users do with it next.

## Child

Imagine you build a toy car from blocks. First you make something with wheels that rolls. Then you add seats, then a steering wheel, then you paint it. If you tried to build the whole car at once, you'd never finish. This project is the blocks, in order — the rolling part first, the pretty parts later.

## Grandma

It's like making a quilt. You don't sew the whole blanket at once — you sew one square, then another, and you lay them out to see the pattern. The first square has to be good, because every other square follows it. This project keeps your squares: the features, the fixes, the tests, and the day a real person used it and smiled.

## Naya

Keep their feature ideas, bugs, tests, links, and users filed under the project. Never invent their product for them; only ask the questions and keep what they give you.

## What It Means

Building an app is not about the technology — it's about the person on the other side of the screen and what changes for them. The mission names the doing: what the product DOES, in one sentence a child could understand. The goal names the smallest working version — the least you can ship that still helps someone. The vision names the everyday change: what life looks like when people use it daily. Features come after the doing, not before it.

## Why It Matters

Most apps die half-built because the builder tried to build everything before anyone used anything. A project forces the small version first: one thing that works, in front of one real user, this week. That first user tells you more in five minutes than a month of planning ever could. This template makes the user the boss of what gets built next.

## Real-World Example

A citizen wants a simple app that reminds her mother to take her medicine — big buttons, one tap, nothing confusing. Her mission: "Remind Mum to take her medicine, with buttons so big she can't get lost." Her goal: "A first version on Mum's phone that reminds her once a day." Her vision: "Mum takes her medicine every day without anyone worrying." Her links hold the apps that inspire her and her sketches; her notes hold every test with Mum; her people hold Mum and her sister. The first morning Mum taps the button herself is the proof it works.

## How It Connects

This template is the business template pointed at a screen: the customer becomes the user, the sale becomes the tap, the proof becomes someone using it every day. The skeleton is the same — mission, goal, vision, links, notes, people — but the product is the proof, and the user is the judge. If a real person uses it, it's real; if nobody uses it, it's a drawing.

## How to Apply

Pick this template, then answer its three questions in your own words. Add your first link (an app you admire, a sketch, a doc). Write your first note (the one thing it must do, in one sentence). Add one person (the first person who will use it). Then build the smallest version that does that one thing — and put it in that person's hands. The project keeps what they do with it, and that tells you what to build next.

## What Changes Now

1. Your product has a home — one project, not scattered sketches and half-started code.
2. The one thing it must do is written down, where you can't forget it for the pretty parts.
3. Every feature idea, bug, and real-user test files into the same place.
4. Your first working version has a date on it — a finish line you can actually reach.
5. The product grows by use, not by guessing: what people do with it decides what comes next.

## What's In It For You

You get to ship something real — small, working, in a real person's hands — instead of dreaming about a perfect app for a year. And you get the record of how it grew: the first version, the first user, the first time someone used it without you watching. That record is the product's memory, and it never forgets what users actually wanted.
