# The Chapter Method

## Nutshell

Tell each story in chapters — 3 or 4 pictures per chapter, each one telling a different part — and never tell the same story twice.

## Child

Imagine a storybook. Each chapter has a few pictures, and every picture shows something NEW — the first picture shows the hero, the second shows the journey, the third shows the treasure. If two pictures showed the exact same thing, that would be boring, right? So each picture must tell its own part of the story, and you should be able to tell which chapter you're in just by looking at the pictures.

## Grandma

Think of a photo album where each page is one part of a holiday — the arrival, the beach, the dinner — and no two pages repeat the same moment. Every page earns its place. And every page is labeled, so you always know which trip you're looking at. That's the chapter method: a story told in chapters, each picture doing a job no other picture does.

## What It Means

Three to four images per chapter (per innovation), 30 to 40 pictures in total. Every batch is labeled with which innovation it represents — within the 3 or 4 pictures you must be able to tell which innovation it is, or there's a problem. No story told twice: each image tells a different part of the chapter.

## Why It Matters

A story told twice is a story wasted, and a chapter nobody can name is a chapter that failed. When every image earns its place, the whole presentation stays tight, every picture pulls its weight, and the person judging it can tell what they're looking at in seconds.

## How It Connects

It connects to the Mirror Pattern, because each chapter follows the same visual rhythm; to the Literal Law, because each picture must be obvious on its own; and to the No-Counting Law, because the pictures are the story, not a tally of pictures.

## How to Apply

For each innovation, make 3 or 4 pictures that each show a different part of it, label the batch clearly with the innovation's name, and check: can someone tell which innovation this is just from these pictures? If not, redo them. If two pictures tell the same part, cut one.

## What's In It For You

You get presentations where nothing repeats and nothing is filler — every picture shows you something new, and you can tell exactly which story you're in. The whole thing stays tight, clear, and worth your time.
