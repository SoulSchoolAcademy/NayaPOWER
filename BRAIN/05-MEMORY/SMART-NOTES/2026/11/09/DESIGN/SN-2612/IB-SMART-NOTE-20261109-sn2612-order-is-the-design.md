## Child

Imagine your bedroom. Your favorite toy goes on the top shelf where you can see it first. Your shoes go by the door, not on the bed. The lamp goes beside the bed, not in the middle of the floor. Nothing is new — the toy, the shoes, the lamp were all there already. But when everything is where it belongs, the room feels right. And when the lamp is in the middle of the floor, you trip over it — even though it's a perfectly good lamp. Where a thing sits is part of what the thing is.

## Grandma

Dear, it's like setting the table. The plate goes in the middle, the fork on the left, the knife on the right, the glass above — not because any one of them is wrong on its own, but because a person sitting down should know, without thinking, where everything is. If you put the glass under the plate, the meal is still the same meal — but nobody can eat it properly. And if you hide the salt behind the flowers, it might as well not be on the table at all. A thing his eye cannot find is a thing that isn't there.

## Why it matters

1. "How many are complete?" is the question every visitor asks with their click, and the page's own answer was wrong by eight — nearly double. A wrong count decides what gets built next (SN-2510).
2. The page was nearly right, and the remaining defects were all about seeing: the Academy in the wrong place, the Ten introduced instead of headlined, a whole section invisible for want of a box. None needed new words; all needed better placement (SN-2511).
3. A proof mechanism that fails silently is worse than no proof mechanism — it reports failure where the system is actually fine, and the next time it reports failure, nobody knows whether to believe it (SN-2512).
4. Gray text is the quietest way a page breaks its own promise. It doesn't crash anything — it just sits there looking like it came from another project, and the reader feels the page is unfinished without knowing why (SN-2513).
5. A page with one real gauge and three fake ones teaches the reader that the real one might be fake too. Reuse fixes the trust problem at its root: if every gauge is the same real gauge, every gauge is believable (SN-2514).
6. Chrome that interrupts is worse than chrome that is merely present — it trains the reader to ignore the bottom of the screen, which is where the bar lives, so the bar defeats its own purpose (SN-2515).
7. Scale is the fastest way a page betrays itself. A visitor who cannot tell whether the numbers are real has already stopped believing the page (SN-2516).
8. Width is the frame the reader trusts. Every section that keeps the frame teaches the reader the page is one thing; every section that breaks it teaches the reader the page is pieces (SN-2517).
