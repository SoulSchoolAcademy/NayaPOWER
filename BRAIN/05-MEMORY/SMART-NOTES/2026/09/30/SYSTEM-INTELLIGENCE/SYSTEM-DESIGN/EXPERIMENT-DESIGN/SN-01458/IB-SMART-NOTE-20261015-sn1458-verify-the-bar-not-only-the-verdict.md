## IN A NUTSHELL
A frozen, pre-registered bar protects the measurement — but not the interpretation. t0616's verbatim P12 beat every arm on all three tasks, and the pre-registered PASS stood. The owed paraphrase control (t2300) then showed: same-length reworded P12 fell to 20/17/24 — dead last on C, the very bar t0616's F2 scored — and P5's non-consequence content at P12's length fell to 22/24/22. The driver was P12's exact surface phrasing, not consequence content, not length. t0616's interpretation ("consequence-shaped content is the judge's edge") was retracted — and the deeper catch is that t0616's own F1 frozen bar embedded the artifact cell (C_principle_track × P12). Before interpreting any frozen-bar edge, audit the bar's cells for embedded confounds: controls must target the bar's cells, not just the finding's headline.

## HUMAN NOTE
Two scoring runs told two stories. The first run froze its scorecard in advance — good science — and declared one principle the winner on all three tasks. The pre-registered result stood: the numbers were honest. But "why did it win?" was never pre-registered. So the lane ran the owed control: the same principle, reworded to the same length. The winner collapsed to dead last. The edge wasn't in the idea — it was in the exact phrasing, baked into the frozen scorecard's own test cell. The lesson for anyone running experiments: freezing the scorecard protects you from moving the goalposts, but it doesn't protect the scorecard itself from carrying the bias. When you win an edge off a frozen bar, reword the winner before you explain it.

## CHILD NOTE
Imagine a spelling test where the teacher writes the answer key before the test. That's fair — nobody can change the answers later. But what if the answer key itself has a mistake baked in? One kid studied the exact sentences from the key and won. When the teacher reworded the sentences, that kid came last. The answer key was fair — but the key itself was the problem. So: check the answer key, not just the scores.

## GRANDMA NOTE
Locking the test questions ahead of time is honest — it stops anyone from changing the questions to favor a winner. But the questions themselves can still carry a hidden tilt. One candidate won because she happened to match the exact wording of the locked questions; when the same questions were reworded, she came last. Lock the questions, yes — but then examine the questions themselves before you celebrate the winner.

## NAYA NOTE
This is the experimental-design refinement of the measurement/interpretation split: pre-registration constrains the measurement step (which arm wins, the numbers) but the interpretation step ("why it won") is post-hoc inference over the frozen bar's own cells. If a cell contains a confound — exact phrasing, position, length — the interpretation inherits it, and no amount of measurement rigor removes it. The t2300 run is the template for the audit: length-matched paraphrase of the winning arm plus a content-swap arm at the winner's length, isolating phrasing vs content vs length. The gap closed was a real one (t0616 F2's "consequence-shaped content is the judge's edge" is now retracted), but the transferable lesson is the bar-cell audit, not the P12 specifics. In any frozen-bar protocol, add one line to the procedure: before interpreting an edge, run the paraphrase-robust cell audit.
