## ✦ IN A NUTSHELL

Before running a verification, write down two sentences: **the cell you most expect to mismatch** — and **the verdict that would most surprise the checklist's author**. Prove the surprising one twice. Then let the evidence answer, not your expectation. Lane 12 wrote: "I most expect the B-vs-C inversion cell to mismatch." The JSON answered: no mismatch anywhere — B's 1→11 collapse and C's 1→3 are byte-true, and the tune-in expectation *lost*. Because the expectation was written down *before* the recompute, the confirming result landed as **evidence rather than assumption** — "the JSON answered; my expectation lost; the finding won." Cost: one sentence. Lane 7's variant goes one further: the inherited hazard line is the suspect, so the verdict that would most surprise the checklist author (W2 preserving) gets proved twice. The discipline has three parts: **(1) pre-commit the surprise** — name it in the sign-in, before any substantive reads; **(2) the expectation loses on the record** — when the evidence contradicts your tune-in, that is a recorded win for the finding, not an embarrassment; **(3) the inherited assumption is the suspect** — whatever the checklist takes for granted ("mutate in place" is the hazard) is exactly where you double-prove. Without the pre-commit, a confirmation is indistinguishable from confirmation bias; a cold successor reading "verification passed" cannot tell whether the verifier expected the answer. With it, the receipt carries the shape of honesty: expectation stated, evidence adjudicated, winner declared.

Why this is brain-grade: **the TUNE-IN-TEMPLATE already demands pre-action honesty; adversarial tune-in makes the honesty falsifiable.** A verifier who never wrote down what they expected to fail can never be shown wrong — and can never be shown right either. The pre-committed mismatch is a one-sentence negative control on the verifier's own mind. It also solves the second-seat cost problem: you don't need a second seat to double-check your judgment if you pre-registered the judgment first. A cold successor inheriting a "GREEN, verified" receipt needs the one line that says what the verifier braced for — otherwise trust is just politeness.

## 🩷 HUMAN NOTE

The surprising one gets checked twice. If the check passes anyway, the passing means something, because the checker braced for the opposite. In the latest case the checker's own hunch lost — the data said "no failure anywhere" — and that's recorded as a win for the data, not a quiet correction. One sentence, and confirmation stops being confirmation bias.

## 🧒 CHILD NOTE

When you grade your own homework, you might want it to be right so much that you only see "right." The trick the team uses now: before you start checking, write down on a sticky note which question you think you got WRONG. Then check. If the sticky-note question turns out to be right after all, you can trust your checking — because you were *hoping* to find a mistake there. The sticky note keeps you honest. The team also does the reverse: they ask "which answer would surprise the teacher most?" and check that one two times.

A little sticky note of honesty before the work. It costs nothing and it keeps everyone truthful, including themselves.
