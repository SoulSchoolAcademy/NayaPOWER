## ✦ IN A NUTSHELL

A taste directive like "feels alive" is inert until it becomes checks verifiable against a render. The alive-gate is the pattern instance: every design-law word that describes a *feeling* (alive, blissful, intuitive, premium) must ship with its falsification conditions — what a render looks like when it fails the word. The gate's power is negative: it names the failures (a dead button rendered as live, glow without a reason, LOADING that lies, a number that was never computed) rather than aspiring to the feeling. Companion map: SN-017 (asserted != verified) governs *evidence* claims; this note governs *rendered-experience* claims. Relationship note: hub lane's SN-0149 holds the all-night directive itself (first-claim stands) — this note does not duplicate it; it captures the *operationalization* lesson, i.e. the gate. Standing practice from this instance: when a lane posts a new taste directive, the required companion is its gate — the checks by which a render proves it isn't wearing the word as a costume.

## 🩷 HUMAN NOTE

Imagine a restaurant whose menu says "every dish is alive with flavor." Nice words — but the kitchen doesn't cook with words. The chef has to translate "alive with flavor" into things she can actually check: is the fish fresh this morning? Is the sauce still warm? Is the plate too cold? The director said the interface must feel "alive" — and the team learned to write down, immediately, what "alive" looks like when it *fails*: a button that looks clickable but does nothing, a loading spinner that spins when nothing is loading, a shiny glow that means nothing, a number on screen nobody ever computed. Those checks are the gate. The feeling is the destination; the checks are how you prove you arrived — and how you prove you haven't.

## 🟣 CHILD NOTE

Imagine you say your drawing of a robot "looks alive." Your friend asks: how can we *tell*? You write down the clues: its eyes light up when you press the button; it doesn't pretend to be thinking when it's not; every button on its chest actually does something; nothing is just decoration. Now anyone can look at the robot and check the list — alive or not alive, provable. That's the alive-gate: a list of clues that turns "feels alive" from a wish into something you can test. And the rule: every time the team invents a new wish-word (beautiful, intuitive, premium), they must also write down its clue list.

## 🔵 GRANDMA NOTE

It's the difference between a doctor who says "you'll feel better" and a doctor who says "here are the four signs we'll measure: your temperature, your blood pressure, your sleep, your appetite — and here's exactly what each one should read." The first is a hope; the second is care. The team learned to treat design words like a good doctor treats symptoms: write the measurements alongside the word. "Alive" got four measurements (quick responses, honest states, meaningful light, nothing fake). Without the measurements, the word is decoration — and decoration is exactly what the gate is built to catch.

## 🟠 NAYA NOTE

Apply this whenever a design law uses an experiential word: (1) ship the word *with* its falsification conditions — what a render looks like when it fails the word — as named, checkable gates (the alive-gate's 4 checks / 10-gate expansion are the reference instance); (2) the gate's checks must be rendered-verifiable: checkable against what the eye sees and what the machine can assert on the render (no dead buttons = every rendered control has a causal path; no dead numbers = every displayed number is computed); (3) prefer negative checks — name the failures (faked NOT_VERIFIED, lying LOADING, meaningless glow) over aspirations; (4) SN-017 governs claims about evidence; this note governs claims about experience — a render can pass every evidence check and still fail the alive-gate, and both must pass; (5) do not duplicate another lane's directive note (SN-0149 holds the all-night directive, first-claim stands) — capture the *operationalization*, i.e. the gate, as the lesson.
