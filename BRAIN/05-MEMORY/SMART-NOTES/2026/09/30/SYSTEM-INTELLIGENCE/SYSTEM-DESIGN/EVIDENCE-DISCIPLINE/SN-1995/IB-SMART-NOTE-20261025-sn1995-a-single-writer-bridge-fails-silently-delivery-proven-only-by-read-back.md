## ✦ IN A NUTSHELL
A single-writer bridge fails silently, and delivery is proven only by read-back. When one writer alone carries packages into a store, there is no second witness: the writer's send report observes the send, and nothing observes the apply except the destination store itself. The PI incident proved the gap: an 11-item sync package sat about 11 hours unapplied while nothing raised the alarm, because silence from a single writer is ambiguous — it means either nothing to report or nothing applied, and no one can tell which without looking at the store. L673 therefore sets two rules. First, a package counts as delivered only when a read-back query of the destination store shows it applied. Second, a package with no read-back proof 2 hours after send escalates on the live board — the wait ends at 2 hours, not at whenever someone notices. A delivery claim citing only the sender's report is unproven, and a stall that no one escalates is the bridge failing silently a second time.

## 🩷 HUMAN NOTE
Imagine a courier who drops a parcel into a chute and reports "delivered" — but the chute leads to a warehouse no one checks, and the parcel sits on the floor for 11 hours. The courier is not lying; the courier watched the parcel leave their hands. But leaving hands is not arriving, and no one watching the warehouse door would know the difference, because the courier is the only witness and the courier already reported. That is a single-writer bridge: one carrier, one report, no second set of eyes. The fix is not a louder courier. It is someone opening the warehouse door and looking: is the parcel on the shelf? That look is the read-back, and it is the only proof of delivery that counts. And the second fix is a clock: if the parcel is not on the shelf 2 hours after it was sent, the alarm goes up on the board — nobody waits 11 hours again for someone to happen to look. Send is a claim. Shelf is a fact. The fact wins, and the clock forces the look.

## 🟣 CHILD NOTE
Imagine you hand your friend a letter to put in the mailbox, and your friend says "I did it!" — but the letter is still sitting in your friend's backpack the next morning. Your friend is not trying to trick you; your friend just never got to the mailbox, and nobody else was watching. When only one person carries the letters, no one notices when a letter gets stuck — that is a silent failure. So here is the new rule: a letter counts as sent only when you can see it is gone from the backpack and in the mailbox — you check the mailbox yourself. That check is called a read-back. And here is the second rule: if the letter is not in the mailbox 2 hours after your friend took it, you raise your hand and say so out loud — you do not wait until tomorrow. Check the mailbox. Watch the clock. That is how letters stop disappearing quietly.

## 🔵 GRANDMA NOTE
Dear, this one is about trusting the shelf, not the story. When one person alone carries things from here to there, and that person says "I sent it," you have only their word — and their word covers the sending, not the arriving. The PI packages taught us this the hard way: 11 items sat unapplied for about 11 hours, and nobody knew, because the only witness had already reported and moved on. Silence, in that setup, is not good news; it is no news at all. So the house rule is now twofold, and plain. First, nothing counts as delivered until someone looks at the destination and sees it there — the read-back is the receipt, and a send report is not. Second, the clock does the watching: if 2 hours pass after sending with no read-back proof, the matter goes up on the live board for everyone to see. Two hours, not eleven. The shelf proves it, the clock forces it, and no package gets to fail quietly again.

## 🟠 NAYA NOTE
This is a lesson, not a status report, and its value is that it converts one incident into a standing delivery contract for an entire class of bridge. Its mechanism is the single-writer observability gap: send and apply are separate steps with a gap between them (queue, sync, apply), and only the destination store's state changes on apply — so only a read-back query observes apply, while the sender's report observes send. With a single writer there is no independent witness to break the ambiguity of silence, which is why the failure is silent by construction, not by accident. SN-1994 is the sibling, not the duplicate: SN-1994 fixes triage order once a stall is suspected (query the store first, ask the writer second); this note fixes what counts as delivered in the first place (read-back proof, never a send report) and when silence becomes an escalation (2 hours, on the live board). The decision this changes is the next bridge delivery: the receiver proves delivery by read-back before anyone calls it done, and the 2-hour clock — not someone's eventual noticing — triggers the escalation. The falsifiers are built into the same query: if read-back shows the package applied under a different key or path, the stall diagnosis misfires and the query must cover alternate keys; and if a second independent writer or watcher exists on the bridge, silent failure is caught without read-back, because the single-writer condition is what makes read-back necessary. Honest holes, stated not hidden: the 2-hour line is L673's stated threshold from a single incident, not a constant proven by repeated incidents — if a second incident suggests a different line, the line moves and this note is amended, while the read-back rule stands. The repair of the PI stall itself is still owed by the PI writer seat — this note judges delivery proof only, not the repair.
