## ✦ IN A NUTSHELL

**When every option on the table agrees on the facts, the decision isn't among them — it's beneath them. Rule the defect, not the corners.** A decision packet's options are framings of one shared fact set; if A, B, and C all accept the same facts, picking a corner just picks a framing. The real variable is the defect the framings share but none names — the unconditioned gate, the unasked question. The ruling pattern is therefore two beats: packet first (an assembler builds the genuinely-decidable options, each with what-decision-it-changes plus for-and-against), ruler second (a different seat tests whether the options disagree on facts — and if they don't, re-derives from the defect itself). Assembler ≠ ruler, always.

Why this is brain-grade: option-picking feels like deciding, but it's often just choosing which frame to wear. The spread-bar ruling proved the stronger move: all three corners agreed the ~30% spread plateau was intrinsic, so the decision wasn't "which corner" — it was the unconditioned gate underneath (a ≤10% bar with no host qualification). Naming the fourth option (the host-qualified bar) resolved what no corner could. A ruler who only picks corners can never find the option nobody framed.

## 🟣 CHILD NOTE

Imagine three friends arguing about which road to take to the lake — left, right, or straight. But they all agree the bridge is out. Picking left, right, or straight doesn't matter if the bridge is out! The smart kid doesn't pick a road — she says "forget the roads, fix the bridge." That's ruling the defect, not the corners: when everyone agrees on the facts, the answer is underneath the choices, not among them.

## 👵 GRANDMA NOTE

It's like three doctors arguing about which medicine to prescribe, when they all agree on the diagnosis but nobody's asking why you got sick in the first place. Choosing among their prescriptions is picking a corner. The wiser move is to ask the question none of them asked — what's causing it? Treat the cause, not the argument.

## 🧠 NAYA NOTE

Make this the standing form for every 'rule on X' brief: (1) an assembler seat builds the decision packet — genuinely-decidable options, each carrying what-decision-it-changes plus honest for-and-against; pin-rot actively hunted, not assumed. (2) A different seat rules — first test: do the options disagree on any fact? If yes, adjudicate the facts. If no, the decision sits beneath the corners: name the shared defect and rule on that, including naming an option nobody framed when the defect demands it. Verdict language follows the measurement: UNKNOWN-by-design where the instrument can't know, never a forced PASS/FAIL. The packet is the instrument; the ruler is the intelligence. Never the same seat.
