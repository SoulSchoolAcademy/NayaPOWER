## IN A NUTSHELL

Score your numbers' epistemics: the 7-dimension scores are judgments, marked as such in the verdict table — arithmetic must not launder them. In the t0762-lane8 DEMOTE verdict (calculus: demote 8.6 / refuse 6.35 / downstream-only 5.3), "the consumer can check" was not a verdict — the bytes showed the FALSIFIED lesson winning by 8.37 points (82.30 vs 73.93), and that margin was quoted as decisive. But the margin is a win inside judgment space: every one of those cells is a judgment, so differencing them yields a judgment with error bars, never a measurement. The doctrine: every verdict table tags each cell JUDGMENT vs MEASURED; any margin, delta, or ranking computed from judgment cells is reported as provisional — aggregation preserves epistemic level, it never raises it. Precision is not certainty. A verdict that hinges on judgment-space arithmetic must say so explicitly in the decision note instead of quoting the margin as proof. (The verdict itself still stood — DEMOTE — but it stands on the reasoning, not on the 8.37.)

## HUMAN NOTE

Imagine three restaurant reviews, each scored 1–10 by a friend. Averaging them to 8.3 and announcing "this is an 8.3-star restaurant, scientifically" is laundering: the average is still just opinions, wearing a lab coat. The honest way is to say "three friends rate it around 8" — same information, no false precision. Scorecards are the same: subtracting two judgment scores doesn't turn them into data.

## CHILD NOTE

If you and your friend both guess how many jelly beans are in the jar, your guesses don't become the real count just because you subtract them. Guessing about guesses is still guessing.

## GRANDMA NOTE

Dear, when everyone at the table is giving their opinion, adding the opinions up doesn't turn them into facts. A careful judge says "this is my best judgment" instead of dressing it in numbers.

## NAYA NOTE

This is the scorecard-reading companion to the no-bare-numbers law: the law says every number carries its "so what"; this says every number carries its epistemic status. Together they make verdict tables honest in both directions — what the number means, and what kind of thing the number is. Related to the 3-pass law's "why not a 10": the gaps named in a scorecard are judgments too, and repairs close them by evidence, not by arithmetic.
