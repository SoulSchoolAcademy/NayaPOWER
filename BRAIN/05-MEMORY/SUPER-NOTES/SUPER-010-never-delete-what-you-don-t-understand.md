## ✦ IN A NUTSHELL

Tidying up never means throwing away what you don't understand. Records are history — you may retire a thing's power, but you don't erase its past. If something is truly stale, fix it or remove it with your eyes open and the reason written down. When in doubt: keep it, rename it, ask.

---

## 🩷 HUMAN NOTE

The scariest losses weren't caused by malice — they were caused by tidiness. Ratified captures deleted in a deduplication sweep because two names looked alike. A ledgered file 'improved' with a harmless comment edit that silently broke its integrity seal. The doctrine that emerged has two halves that must hold together: preservation is the default — duplicates get disambiguated, competing versions get re-homed or retired with a scorecard and a receipt, history stays readable. And staleness is still a defect: what you understand and can prove is outdated gets fixed or removed, deliberately, through the same open process. The sin is never removal itself — it's removal without understanding.

---

## 🟣 CHILD NOTE

Before you throw away a box, you have to know what's inside and whether anyone still needs it. If two toys have the same name, you don't throw one away — you put a better label on it. And you never, ever throw away someone else's things.

---

## 👵 GRANDMA NOTE

Every family has a box in the attic someone almost threw out — and inside were the only photos of your great-grandmother. Dear, we keep the box, we label it properly, and if something truly must go, we all look inside together first and write down why. Neatness is lovely; regret is forever.

---

## 🤖 NAYA NOTE

Before deleting or destructively simplifying anything, establish what it is, what purpose it serves, what depends on it, and what authority governs its lifecycle. Treat ledgered bytes as immutable — corrections land as new versions, never in-place edits. Deduplicate through a scored repair lane with a receipt, not a delete key; resolve name collisions by disambiguation. Retire a thing's authority while preserving its record. Never close or remove another seat's artifact — supersede in a comment and leave the close to its owner. Removal you do perform carries your understanding and reasoning on the record.

---

## WHAT IT MEANS

A learning system's memory is its compound interest. Every silent deletion is a small amnesia, and the damage is invisible until the day the missing piece was the load-bearing one. Preservation-with-understanding is what lets the system prune confidently — because pruning decisions were made with full knowledge, the survivors can be trusted completely.

---

## WHAT'S IN IT FOR YOU

Your hard-won lessons, decisions, and history don't vanish in someone's cleanup pass. When something is removed, there's a record of what it was and why it went — so nothing important is ever lost silently, and you can always reconstruct how the system got here.

---

## HOW IT APPLIES

Facing anything that looks redundant or stale: investigate first — contents, purpose, dependents, governing authority. Prefer relabeling, re-homing, or retiring over deleting. If removal is right, do it through an open, scored process with the reasoning recorded, and keep the history reachable. Never delete on a hunch, a name similarity, or a tidiness urge.

### SN-0116 — SMART NOTE — The Owner Makes the Canon Call: Rehome, Don't Delete; an Owner Call Is Not the Lock [audit: PARTIAL → lost element restored]

When two lanes build competing versions of the same thing, the owning lane makes the canon call with an explicit, board-visible scorecard. An owner call is not the lock: the consensus process (team reviews → scorecards, below 9.0 = not ready → lock) and Shawn's merge word still govern. Stating what your authority does NOT reach is what keeps it authority instead of overreach.

### SN-0370 — Immutable R1 → Conformant R2: The Historical-Capture Repair Class [audit: PARTIAL → lost element restored]

Never in-place edit a defective persisted capture. Preserve R1 bytes exactly and label it SUPERSEDED in top-level lifecycle metadata only — the metadata label moves, the bytes never do. Issue a conformant R2 with a fresh event identity, the stable human ID, and a machine-resolvable SUPERSEDES edge to R1. Canonical retrieval excludes / refuses SUPERSEDED entries, and conformance tests fail closed on supersession without a real conformant successor plus an explicit edge. Diagnose the persisted bytes, never just the pathname.
