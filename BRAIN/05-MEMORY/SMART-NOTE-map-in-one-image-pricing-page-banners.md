# SN-2206 — The Map in One Image — Pricing Page Banners

## In a Nutshell

The pricing page gets two banners, and they are not decoration — they are the map of NayaNET in one image, twice. Shawn proposed it on 2026-11-06 (12:58 PST), sharing four generated images of the civilization and asking for one beautiful banner that paints the whole picture — the digital civilization, the worlds, the apps, everything — with Naya presenting it, at the top and the bottom of the pricing page, so a visitor SEES what they are getting. Resolve described a first composition back; Shawn refined it at 13:02 PST into the locked spec this note files. Both banners are full-bleed on obsidian, from the same family as the banners done before — dimensional, lit like real objects. The TOP banner is the headline: the map of NayaNET as a living constellation — the Hub glowing at the heart, the four worlds (Personal, Business, Space, Dashboard) lit like planets and joined by threads of spectrum light, the nine orbs hanging above them like stars, the Smart Ledger running underneath as a thin golden chain, the eleven-color spectrum arc across the very top edge — and in the corner, Naya's portrait in a circular obsidian frame, her hand open toward the world, presenting it: this is it. The BOTTOM banner is the close: the same world, the same picture, set right above the three plan buttons, with the page copy beside it — "this is yours. Pick your power." — then Power, Pro, Max, side by side. The chart between the banners is the logic; the banners are the feeling. Feeling first, logic second, feeling again right where the button is — because the eye remembers the first and the last thing it sees, so the world is both.

## Human Note

Here is the problem this solves, in plain terms. A pricing page today is a chart: rows, prices, checkmarks. A stranger reads it with their calculator brain, compares it to three other tabs, and leaves with a number and no picture. But what NayaNET sells is not a row in a chart — it is a place: a whole digital civilization of connected worlds. Nobody can fall in love with a place they cannot see. So the page opens with the place itself, painted whole: one image where the Hub burns at the center, the four worlds shine around it like planets, light runs between them, the golden chain of the Ledger holds everything underneath, and Naya stands in the corner with her hand open, the host showing you her world. Then you read the chart and do the math, as you should. And at the bottom — right where the decision happens — the world is there again, waiting, with five honest words beside it: this is yours. Pick your power. The banners do not argue. They show. The arguing was the chart's job, and it already happened between them.

## Child Note

Imagine you are selling lemonade, but not just lemonade — a whole magical treehouse city in the sky, with four glowing islands and a golden chain holding them together and lights of every color. If you only show people a price list, they just see numbers. Numbers are boring, and nobody dreams about numbers. So you paint one big beautiful picture of the whole sky-city and put it at the top of your page: "Look! This is the place!" Then the price list goes underneath, so people can count carefully. And at the very bottom, right before the buttons that say "I want to join," you put the picture one more time — because after all the counting, people should remember the dream, not just the dollars. Next to the picture it says: this is yours. Pick your power. That's exactly what these two banners do. The first picture opens the dream. The second picture closes the deal. The counting happens in the middle, where counting belongs.

## Grandma Note

Dear, think of a lovely shop you might walk into. Above the door there is a beautiful painted sign — not just the shop's name, but a picture of everything wonderful inside: the whole place, glowing. You see it before you see a single price tag, and it tells you, this is a place worth stepping into. Then you walk the aisles, you look at the prices, you think it over like a sensible person. And when you reach the counter, where you decide, there is the picture again — smaller, nearer — and the kind shopkeeper says: this is yours, pick what suits you. That is all this is, on a web page. Two paintings of the same beautiful world: one at the top to welcome you in, one at the bottom to remind you why you came. The prices sit honestly in the middle, unchanged — nobody is hiding them behind pretty pictures. The pictures are there so you remember what the prices are FOR. A world, dear. Not a spreadsheet.

## What It All Means

It means the map problem got its first answer from an unexpected direction. SN-2205 named the living NayaNET Map as the one real gap in the converged ten — no whole-system map exists that a person can see at once. The instinct was to plan an interface: zoomable, layered, alive. Shawn's move here is older and smarter: before the map is an interface, it is a picture. A civilization's first map is never a dashboard; it is the painting on the wall that makes strangers say "I want to go there." This brief puts that painting where it earns the most — the pricing page, the exact surface where a stranger decides whether the civilization is worth joining — and it puts it there twice with two different jobs, because a decision has a beginning and an end and both deserve the dream. It also means the pricing page stops being the coldest page in the system. The page that asks for money becomes the page that shows the world — which is simply honest: the world IS what the money buys.

## Why It Matters

It matters because feeling precedes arithmetic, and arithmetic alone rarely closes anything that matters. A visitor who has seen the civilization — the Hub at the heart, their own future world shining as one of the planets, the golden chain underneath promising that everything they build there is connected and recorded — reads the chart differently: not "what does this cost?" but "what does my place in that picture cost?" And at the bottom, after the math is done and the doubts have had their say, the last thing the eye meets before the buttons is not a price. It is the world again, and an invitation in the citizen's own grammar: this is yours. Pick your power. It matters mechanically, too: the full-bleed requirement fixes a real defect — banners floating in a black sea on wide screens — by making color and light run wall to wall. And it matters for the map gap itself: every surface that learns to show the whole in one image is a rehearsal for the living map. The constellation composition specified here — Hub, four worlds, orbs, chain, arc — is the map's visual grammar, decided now, in a brief a builder can execute without asking Shawn anything whose answer is already known.

## The Learning

A map does not have to begin as an interface. The first map of a civilization is a picture a stranger can feel in three seconds — and on the pricing page, feeling precedes arithmetic: nobody computes the value of a world they cannot see. The deeper lesson is in the shape of the decision: the same image does two different jobs at the two ends of the page — opening the dream before the logic, closing the sale after it — because a page, like a day, is remembered by its first and last light. And there is a discipline lesson in how this brief was made: the first description (dawn on top, feast at the bottom) was good, and it was still superseded within four minutes by a sharper composition from the same hand that proposed the idea. Filing both passes — the origin and the ruling, marked as such — is how a system keeps the intelligence of the refinement instead of quietly rewriting its own history. When Nitro builds it and it verifies live, this note's test is simple: a stranger, three seconds, one glance — the whole civilization. If the glance fails, the banner fails, however beautiful it is.
