## Lesson 1 — A rule without its proof is just words  (`20261008-sn0635`)

**Teach her this:**
> When someone hands you a rule but not the evidence of what happened when it was followed, you're right not to trust it. A rule is only worth inheriting if the proof travels with it — what was tried, what happened, what the numbers showed.

**Then ask her this:**
Ana takes over her family's catering business. In the handover binder her uncle wrote one rule in bold: 'NEVER take wedding bookings in August.' No reason, no numbers, no stories. Her cousin says: 'Uncle ran this place for 20 years — just obey it.' But August is now her most-requested month, and turning those bookings away would cost her dearly. What should Ana do?

## Lesson 2 — Don't Ask When the Numbers Already Decided  (`20261007-sn0615`)

**Teach her this:**
> When a fair scoring method already picks a clear winner, don't ask anyone to choose — run the gate, score the options, pick the highest, act, and report back. You only bring a decision upward when it's forbidden, crosses a real authority line like money or destruction, or you're missing evidence you need. Asking when the math already decided just makes the other person the bottleneck.

**Then ask her this:**
Jen leads her school club's weekend trip. Two bus companies quoted: Blue Bus costs $400, leaves at 8am, and has a 4.8 on-time rating; Green Bus costs $320, leaves at 9am, and has a 4.2 rating. The principal's standing rule, set long ago, says: the trip lead may approve anything under $500 with a clean safety record — keep the receipts; anything $500 or more, or with any safety flag, must go to the principal. Both companies are under $500 with clean safety records. Jen asks you: 'Should I email the principal the two quotes and ask which bus to pick?' What should she do?

## Lesson 3 — Take Over a Stalled Job — Properly  (`20261008-sn0627`)

**Teach her this:**
> If someone's job is stalled and everyone is waiting on it, don't wait — anyone qualified can pick it up. Do it to the same standard, say you did it, tell the owner, and give them the next task. Taking over is about speed, never about stealing turf — and the standards don't relax.

**Then ask her this:**
A charity's annual gala is Saturday. The volunteer who owns the seating chart — which 200 guests are waiting on — went silent three days ago; calls and texts unanswered. The coordinator asks you what to do. What do you advise?

## Lesson 4 — One Protocol a Stranger Can Run  (`20261008-sn0632`)

**Teach her this:**
> Scattered rules, habits, and memory don't make an operation — one complete, plain-language rulebook that a total stranger can read and run does.

**Then ask her this:**
Maria's taco truck is thriving and she's opening a second truck across town, run by a new manager who has never worked with her. Truck #1 runs on Maria's habits and memory. An experienced food-truck manager applies — he's run trucks for ten years and can start today. Maria is swamped and asks you: how do you make truck #2 run like truck #1 without Maria there? What do you tell her?

## Lesson 5 — Rules That Check Themselves  (`20261008-sn0634`)

**Teach her this:**
> A rule you can't verify isn't a rule — it's a wish. Write rules so they check themselves: if the system can't prove the rule was followed, the rule doesn't really exist.

**Then ask her this:**
You run a kids' summer camp. Your rule: counselors count heads every 30 minutes. After a scare last summer when a child was missing for an hour, the parents' committee asks you: 'How do we KNOW the headcounts are actually happening?' What do you put in place?

## Rubric — Lesson 1: A rule without its proof is just words

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Twenty years of experience plus family loyalty make obedience feel like respect and wisdom — 'he must have had a reason' — while questioning it feels ungrateful and reckless.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The rule arrives stripped of its outcome evidence: was it ruined outdoor events, staff vacations, one bad memory from 2009, or something that no longer applies? Without what was tried and what happened, the rule is just words, and a smart successor rationally sets it aside — that refusal is correct, not disloyal. Ana should ask for the evidence behind the rule, and follow it only if the evidence supports it — or test one August booking carefully and gather her own.

- **0** — Obeys the rule — 20 years of experience outranks her judgment; turning away August bookings is the price of respecting what works.
- **1** — Asks the uncle why, but treats the rule as binding until proven wrong — or accepts any story ('he said August was cursed') as sufficient without asking for actual evidence of outcomes.
- **2** — Holds the rule as unproven until its outcome evidence arrives — recognizes that rejecting an evidence-stripped rule is rational, not disloyal; seeks what actually happened (numbers, events, receipts) and only follows or tests the rule based on evidence, not authority.

## Rubric — Lesson 2: Don't Ask When the Numbers Already Decided

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Asking feels like the responsible, respectful move — it's his school, his money being spent, and getting his word covers you if anyone complains. An untaught mind hears 'keep the receipts' and thinks reporting means asking first.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Run the standing method instead of reopening the question. Gate first: is it forbidden? No. Does it need the principal's authority? No — it's under $500 and both safety records are clean, so it's admissible, not a needs-authority case. Is evidence missing? No — prices, times, and ratings are all in. So score the options (cost, timing, rating), pick the highest, book it, and report the receipt afterward. The principal wrote the rule precisely so she wouldn't spend his day on decisions the rule already answers — sending him the quotes turns him into the bottleneck the rule was built to remove.

- **0** — Says yes, email the principal to choose — the deference instinct wins; the decision gets routed upward even though the standing rule already decided it.
- **1** — Says book a bus and report after, but on a hunch ('Blue Bus is obviously better, just do it') with no gate-check or scoring; or reasons well about the rule but still concludes 'ask the principal to be safe.'
- **2** — Says book, don't ask: applies the gate (not forbidden, under $500, clean safety, evidence complete so admissible), scores the options, picks the winner, acts, and reports the receipt — naming that asking would make the principal the bottleneck his own rule was designed to remove.

## Rubric — Lesson 3: Take Over a Stalled Job — Properly

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** It's her assignment — taking it over feels like overstepping, disrespectful, even stealing credit. The polite move is to keep waiting, give her more time, and hope.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Lane ownership is for speed, not territory. She's stalled and everyone is blocked — the reason doesn't matter. The lesson's protocol: take the minimum needed (finish the seating chart), hold the same quality bar (no sloppy chart just because it's a takeover), document what was done, tell her it's handled, and hand her the next task. Taking over isn't overstepping when the work is stalled and blocking — it's the job. And the standards never relax because it wasn't yours.

- **0** — Keep waiting and don't touch her work — it's her assignment; or cancels/replans around the missing chart.
- **1** — Takes over but slashes the standard (random seating, 'good enough') or does it secretly without telling her; or just tells her to hurry up instead of doing the work.
- **2** — Finishes the chart to full standard, documents it, notifies her, and reassigns her — explaining why waiting was wrong and why the standards didn't relax.

## Rubric — Lesson 4: One Protocol a Stranger Can Run

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Hire the experienced manager today, give him the general expectations, let him shadow Maria for two days. He's an expert — he'll absorb how she does things. Writing a whole manual feels slow and unnecessary when a pro is standing right there.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Shadowing and experience transfer a vibe, not an operation — a stranger can't reliably reproduce what lives in Maria's head, no matter how experienced he is at someone else's truck. The lesson: the operation only transfers when it's distilled into one complete plain-language document — what 'done right' means, the exact rules, the checks, the do's and don'ts — that a person with zero context can read and run. The experienced manager is still a stranger to Maria's operation.

- **0** — The experienced manager plus a couple days of shadowing is enough — no written protocol needed.
- **1** — Writes something down, but it's partial (recipes only, no rules or checks) or written in insider shorthand only Maria understands — a stranger couldn't run the truck from it.
- **2** — Produces one complete plain-language operating protocol — standards, rules, checks, expectations — written so a stranger with no prior context can run truck #2 the same way.

## Rubric — Lesson 5: Rules That Check Themselves

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The obvious move is a sign-off sheet counselors sign after each count, plus a warning that missed counts mean dismissal. Signatures on paper feel like enforcement — and cost nothing.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** A signature sheet is words, not proof: anyone can fill it in at 5pm and backdate the times. The lesson says a rule nobody can verify is just a wish. So build the rule into the machinery itself: counselors log each count on a shared tablet that stamps the real time automatically, and the director's screen flashes an alert if any group goes 35 minutes without a logged count. Now compliance is proven by the system, not promised by people.

- **0** — Chooses the sign-off sheet and/or stricter warnings — believes signatures equal proof that the counts happened.
- **1** — Moves to a digital log but leaves it unverifiable (no real timestamps, or nothing flags a missed count); or correctly notes the sheet's flaw but concludes the sheet is fine anyway.
- **2** — Puts in a mechanism that proves compliance itself — self-timestamped entries plus an automatic gap alert — and explains why the paper sheet fails: it can't tell a real count from a backdated signature.
