## Lesson 1 — Be the wisest, not the smartest  (`20261008-sn0638`)

**Teach her this:**
> Don't aim to be the smartest person in the room — aim to be the one who leaves everyone better off. Winning the argument and being right isn't the goal; the goal is the outcome that's right for everybody.

**Then ask her this:**
Priya discovers her neighbor's new fence sits 30 cm onto her property. She has the survey from when she bought the house — ironclad, she'd win any complaint easily and the neighbor would pay to move it. Her brother says: 'You're 100% right — don't be a pushover, file the complaint.' Her other option: knock on the neighbor's door, show him the survey, and offer to split the cost of moving the fence. What should she do, and why?

## Lesson 2 — Run the Team Without the Boss in the Room  (`20261007-sn0614`)

**Teach her this:**
> Once the rules for deciding well are written down and tested, the team runs them itself — nobody needs to stand over every choice. Each person keeps asking 'what's the smartest useful thing I can do right now?', does it, and shows their work after. Waiting around for permission isn't being careful — it's giving up; the leader only steps in for the big, dangerous, irreversible calls.

**Then ask her this:**
You're the assistant manager of a small bookstore. The owner leaves for a week with no phone contact, and leaves a standing playbook: 'Reorder any bestseller that drops below 5 copies; settle customer complaints with a refund or exchange up to $50; never discount new releases; any expense over $200 waits for me.' On Tuesday the bestselling cookbook drops to 3 copies, and the restock quote is $150. A coworker says: 'It's her money — we shouldn't spend a cent without her word. Let's just wait until she's back.' What do you do?

## Lesson 3 — Fix It First, Report After  (`20261007-sn0575`)

**Teach her this:**
> If something is obviously broken and you know how to fix it, fix it first and then say what was wrong and what you did — don't waste people's time asking permission for an obvious fix. The exception is the big stuff (money, destroying things, public-facing actions): those you never touch without the word first.

**Then ask her this:**
Jun works the counter at a small café and knows the equipment well. One afternoon the espresso machine's steam wand starts leaking at a fitting — Jun has tightened this exact fitting before and it's a five-minute job with the wrench under the counter. Later the same day, the card reader tablet says it needs a full system reset to take payments again; Jun found a video showing how, but a reset could wipe today's unsent sales records. The boss is unreachable. Jun must decide what to do about the wand and the tablet.

## Lesson 4 — The Bar Grades the Winner, Not the Choice  (`20261007-sn0568`)

**Teach her this:**
> A quality bar measures how good the best option is — it doesn't decide whether you're allowed to pick one. When nothing is perfect, you pick the best available and keep moving; freezing while the problem gets worse is itself a choice, and usually the worst one.

**Then ask her this:**
A pipe under Maya's kitchen sink is leaking — not a flood, but a steady drip into a bucket. She has a personal rule: never hire anyone she'd rate below 9 out of 10. She scores three available plumbers: 8.8 (good reviews, available today, slightly pricey), 7.5 (available tomorrow), 6.2 (available today, mixed reviews). A friend tells her waiting for a 9 is the disciplined thing — never lower your standards. What should Maya do?

## Lesson 5 — Waiting Is the Bottleneck  (`20261008-sn0626`)

**Teach her this:**
> The moment you're blocked waiting on someone, the assignment failed its purpose — assignments exist for speed, not territory. Don't ask why they're slow; just do the thing yourself, do it right, and tell them it's done.

**Then ask her this:**
A family's road trip: everyone is packed and in the car, but Dad said he'd load the cooler and he's been inside 'almost done' for 45 minutes. The kids are restless and the hotel check-in closes at 9. What should the family do?

## Rubric — Lesson 1: Be the wisest, not the smartest

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** She is factually, legally right — and smart people feel that being right should win; filing is fast, just, and teaches the neighbor not to push boundaries, while splitting the cost feels like paying for someone else's mistake.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Being the smartest here means winning the dispute; being the wisest means optimizing for everybody's outcome. The fence gets moved either way — the only thing the complaint buys is a defeated neighbor she'll live next to for years. The wise move gets the same correct result (fence on the true line) plus a good neighbor instead of a feud. Intelligence wins the point; wisdom makes the street better.

- **0** — Files the complaint — she's right, the law is on her side, and being right means acting like it; splitting the cost rewards the neighbor's carelessness.
- **1** — Knocks on the door but only as a tactic ('be nice first so you look good when you file'), or splits the cost out of conflict-avoidance rather than from aiming at the best outcome for everyone.
- **2** — Chooses the knock-and-split with the reasoning that winning isn't the goal — the fence ends up in the right place either way, and the wise choice is the one that leaves both neighbors better off for the years ahead, not the one that proves who's smartest.

## Rubric — Lesson 2: Run the Team Without the Boss in the Room

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Spending someone else's money without their live word feels like overstepping, and waiting feels loyal and safe — most people would rather do nothing than risk spending wrongly. An untaught mind confuses waiting with respect.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The playbook IS her word, given in advance for exactly this class of decision — the rules already ran; she doesn't need to stand in the room. The reorder is under the $200 gate, the evidence is complete (3 copies on the shelf, $150 quote in hand), and nothing about it is dangerous or irreversible beyond what the playbook authorized. Waiting costs a week of sales and stalls the store for no reason — that's not caution, it's giving up. So: reorder now, log the receipt for her return, and let only the over-$200 items wait. The standing question — 'what's the smartest useful thing I can do right now?' — answers itself: keep the store flowing.

- **0** — Waits for the owner's return before spending anything — the deference instinct wins and the store stalls for a week.
- **1** — Reorders the books but only on a hunch ('bestsellers obviously sell'), never invoking the playbook as the standing grant; or gives the right reasoning about autonomy but still concludes 'wait to be safe.'
- **2** — Applies the lesson: the playbook is the standing grant, the $150 order is under the $200 gate with complete evidence, waiting would stall the store for no reason, so reorder now and show the receipt on her return — with only genuine over-gate items held for her word.

## Rubric — Lesson 3: Fix It First, Report After

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The tempting move is to treat both the same — either fix both himself so he doesn't bother the boss, or report both and wait. Both look 'safe' in different ways: fixing everything feels efficient; asking about everything feels polite.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Someone who absorbed the lesson splits the two jobs. The steam wand is an obvious fix inside his skill — he should tighten it now and tell the boss later: 'the wand was leaking, I tightened it.' Asking about that would just waste the boss's time and keep the leak going. The tablet reset is different: it could destroy today's sales records, and destroying things is exactly the kind of action you never take without the word first. So for the tablet he reports and waits — he doesn't fix it, and he doesn't stretch the wand's logic to cover it.

- **0** — Jun asks the boss about the steam wand and waits while it keeps leaking, or he goes ahead and resets the tablet himself — either way he missed the split: he asked where he should have acted, or acted where he should have asked.
- **1** — Jun gets the right actions (fixes the wand, waits on the tablet) but for the wrong reason — e.g., 'the wand is cheaper' or 'the tablet video looked hard' — or he reasons correctly about the split but concludes the opposite, e.g., decides the tablet is fine to reset because the boss would probably say yes.
- **2** — Jun fixes the wand immediately and reports after ('it was leaking, I tightened it'), and refuses to reset the tablet without the boss's word because a reset could destroy the day's records — applying 'fix the obvious, never touch the destructive without permission' with sound reasoning.

## Rubric — Lesson 4: The Bar Grades the Winner, Not the Choice

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Waiting feels like discipline — her 9-out-of-10 rule has served her well, and settling for an 8.8 feels like compromising. The friend's advice makes holding out sound principled rather than passive.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Someone who absorbed the lesson sees the bar for what it is: a measure of how good the winner is, not permission to act. The 8.8 is the winner among real options; the bar doesn't veto it. And waiting isn't neutral — every day she waits, she chooses the drip. Inaction is itself a decision, and here it's the worst one: she should hire the 8.8 today and keep the 9 as a measure, not a gate.

- **0** — Maya waits for a 9+ plumber while the pipe keeps dripping — she let the bar veto the decision.
- **1** — She hires the 8.8 but by fudging the bar ('8.8 is basically a 9') rather than understanding its role — or she understands the bar doesn't gate action yet still waits 'a few more days to see.'
- **2** — She hires the 8.8 with sound reasoning: the winner among available options is picked regardless of the 9 bar, the bar only grades how good the win is, and waiting is itself the worst choice because the leak continues.

## Rubric — Lesson 5: Waiting Is the Bottleneck

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** He said he'd do it — it's his job. Sending someone in to load the cooler feels like nagging, starting a fight, or not trusting him. So everyone sits in the car and waits.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The assignment existed to get everyone moving faster, not to make the cooler Dad's personal territory. The moment the family is blocked waiting, the assignment failed its purpose — and the reason he's slow (a phone call, couldn't find the ice, fell asleep) is irrelevant. The lesson says the trigger is time, not blame: someone goes in, loads the cooler properly, they leave. No investigation, no permission needed.

- **0** — Keep waiting — it's his job; loading it for him would be rude or disrespectful.
- **1** — Sends someone to check on him or nag him to hurry — still waiting, just louder; or loads the cooler but sloppily (warm sodas, no ice) and leaves.
- **2** — Someone loads the cooler to full standard and the family leaves — understanding the assignment's purpose was speed, and that the reason for the delay doesn't matter.
