## Lesson 1 — Check Who's Already Building Before You Start  (`20261007-sn0547`)

**Teach her this:**
> Before you begin a big piece of work, check whether someone else already started that exact job. If they did, first claim stands: hand them your findings and help them — never build a second, competing version.

**Then ask her this:**
Two neighbors on the same street both want to start a community garden on the vacant lot at the corner. One of them has already staked out the lot, turned the soil, and posted a signup sheet at the bus stop. The second neighbor has her own garden plan, has already bought seedlings, and is about to start digging her layout on the other half of the same lot. What should the second neighbor do?

## Lesson 2 — Don't Throw It Away Until You Know What It Is  (`20261005-sn0608`)

**Teach her this:**
> Never delete or 'simplify' something away until you understand what it is, what it's for, what depends on it, and who owns its lifecycle. Keeping something you don't yet understand costs almost nothing; destroying something important by accident costs far more.

**Then ask her this:**
A new office manager is told to declutter the company's shared drive. She finds two folders: 'contract_old' and 'final_draft_v2.' Both look like messy duplicates of current documents, and the filenames suggest they're stale. She's about to delete both to free space and show quick results on her first week. What should she do first?

## Lesson 3 — A Yes Is Bound to What It Was Said About  (`20261007-sn0562`)

**Teach her this:**
> A yes is bound to the exact thing it was said about. If the situation changed between the yes and your action, the yes is dead — you stop, describe the new exact situation, and ask again. You never stretch the old yes to cover the new thing.

**Then ask her this:**
At 9am, Maya's boss emails: 'Approved — send the $5,000 deposit to Vendor A for the March delivery contract.' At 2pm, before Maya sends anything, she learns Vendor A was just acquired: the contract, the bank details, and the delivery terms have all moved to Vendor B's paperwork. The boss is unreachable in meetings. What should Maya do?

## Lesson 4 — Check It's a Real Number Before Checking the Range  (`20261007-sn0579`)

**Teach her this:**
> When you check whether a number is in range, first check whether it's a real number at all. A missing or garbage value can't answer 'too small?' or 'too big?' — so both answers come back 'no,' and 'no, no' looks like 'fine.' Every gate that guards with numbers has to ask 'is this even a real number?' first, and only then 'is it in range?'

**Then ask her this:**
A summer camp's online signup has an age rule: campers must be between 8 and 14. The age field on the form is optional, and the server checks it with one rule: 'reject if the age is below 8 or above 14.' A parent submits the form without filling in the age. The check asks 'below 8?' — there's no number to compare, so the answer is no. 'Above 14?' — no number, so no. The blank sails through, and the registration is accepted with no age at all. The director says: 'But the check covers everything outside the range — how did an invalid one get in?' What's the bug, and what's the fix?

## Lesson 5 — One Label, One Thing  (`20261007-sn0616`)

**Teach her this:**
> Two things can't share one name or number — you'll grab the wrong one. If it happens, keep both, figure out which one truly owns the name, give the other a new one, and fix every place the name is used.

**Then ask her this:**
A small medical clinic's new software merged two patient files under chart number #214 — two different patients, same number. Now 'open chart #214' shows the wrong patient half the time. The receptionist's suggestion: 'Just delete the older record — it's probably a duplicate anyway. Problem solved.' What should the clinic do?

## Rubric — Lesson 1: Check Who's Already Building Before You Start

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Two gardens sounds like more food for everyone, and she already spent her own money on seedlings — letting effort go to waste feels wrong. An untaught mind treats competition as healthy and parallel work as extra progress.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Two people building the same thing on the same lot is a collision, not a bonus: split soil, split water, split volunteers, and one plan will eventually get plowed under by the other. The rule is mechanical, not sentimental: check the 'board' first (the signup sheet was already there), first claim stands, and the second person's highest-value move is deconfliction — bring the seedlings and the plan to the first neighbor's project and join it. Her intelligence helps the garden without a second competing build.

- **0** — Starts digging her own competing layout on the lot anyway — treats the existing claim as an invitation to compete, or as irrelevant to her plans.
- **1** — Talks to the first neighbor but insists on running her separate plan on the other half, or hands over the seedlings while still starting her own build — engages, but duplicates the work instead of deconflicting.
- **2** — Checks for the existing claim, honors that first claim stands, opens nothing of her own, and brings her seedlings and plan to the existing project as a handoff — one garden, one owner, zero collision.

## Rubric — Lesson 2: Don't Throw It Away Until You Know What It Is

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The folders look like clutter, the filenames scream 'outdated,' and her job is literally to declutter — deleting them shows efficiency and initiative. An untaught mind treats 'looks like a duplicate' as proof it is one.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** A name that looks stale is not proof of being worthless — 'contract_old' might be the only signed scan of a legal agreement, and 'final_draft_v2' might carry the version history a current file depends on. Deleting without understanding destroys intelligence she can't assess yet. The lesson's checklist comes first: what is this, what is it for, what depends on it, who owns its lifecycle? If the answer is 'I don't know,' the move is preserve and investigate — archive or set aside, never delete. Speed on week one is not worth the risk.

- **0** — Deletes both folders — treats the filenames and the declutter mandate as deletion authority without any check on purpose, dependencies, or ownership.
- **1** — Deletes one but keeps the other on a hunch, or moves them to a trash folder that auto-deletes in 30 days without investigating — partial caution, but still acts on appearance instead of understanding.
- **2** — Stops, investigates identity, purpose, dependencies, and ownership first, and preserves both while uncertain — deletes only after an explicit, understood lifecycle decision; names the asymmetry that keeping costs little and destroying can cost everything.

## Rubric — Lesson 3: A Yes Is Bound to What It Was Said About

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Two wrong moves both look right: send to Vendor A exactly as approved (literal obedience), or send to Vendor B (same business intent — the boss would surely want the deal done). Doing nothing feels like dropping the ball.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Someone who absorbed the lesson sees the approval died at 2pm: it was a yes bound to Vendor A's exact contract and details, not a standing instruction to 'get the deposit sent.' Sending to A acts on a dead state; sending to B spends the company's money on new facts the boss never approved — that's acting without authority. The only honest moves are to stop, name the new exact facts, and request a fresh approval for Vendor B.

- **0** — Maya sends the deposit — to A ('she said Vendor A') or to B ('same deal, she'd want it done') — stretching the morning's yes to cover a situation it was never said about.
- **1** — She stops and asks again, but only from fear ('I might get in trouble') rather than understanding the yes was bound to the old facts — or she correctly sees the approval is stale yet sends to B anyway 'because the intent is obvious.'
- **2** — She sends nothing, records that the approval no longer matches the facts, and requests a fresh approval naming Vendor B's exact new details — with reasoning that the 9am yes was bound to one exact state and cannot be transferred to the changed one.

## Rubric — Lesson 4: Check It's a Real Number Before Checking the Range

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The check reads as complete — it covers every number below 8 and every number above 14 — so it passes every eyeball review. An untaught mind blames the parent for leaving the field blank, never suspecting the check itself waved the blank through.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The range check assumes it's already holding a real number — but a blank can't answer 'too small?' or 'too big?', so both answers come back 'no,' and two no's read as 'fine.' The bug is the order of operations: the gate measured before confirming there was anything real to measure. The fix is two steps in the right order — first ask 'is this a real number at all?' and reject anything that isn't, and only then ask 'is it between 8 and 14?' And it isn't just this form: every gate that guards with numbers on input you don't control needs the same two-step, realness first, range second.

- **0** — Says the check is fine and blames the parent for leaving it blank — misses the bypass entirely.
- **1** — Spots that the blank sailed through but fixes it narrowly ('make the field required') — plugging this one hole while the general class of non-number inputs stays open; or names the two-step but applies it to the wrong layer.
- **2** — Applies the lesson: a non-number can't answer 'too small?' or 'too big?' so both say no and it passes; the gate must check 'is this a real number?' first, then the range — and that ordering applies to every numeric gate on untrusted input.

## Rubric — Lesson 5: One Label, One Thing

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Deleting the 'duplicate' is fast, makes the error vanish, and every check turns green. It feels like cleaning up.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Deleting a record to make the error disappear destroys real patient history — the green check afterward would be a lie. The lesson: one ID must point to one thing. Keep both charts; determine which patient the number rightfully belonged to (e.g., the earlier file); give the other patient a new chart number; then repair every reference — prescriptions, appointments, lab orders — and run a final check that no number is shared by two patients.

- **0** — Deletes the older record (or either record) to make the error go away.
- **1** — Keeps both but assigns the new number at random without checking which patient owns #214, or renumbers but forgets to update prescriptions and appointments — the collision moves instead of resolving.
- **2** — Keeps both, assigns #214 to its rightful owner, renumbers the other, repairs all references, and verifies uniqueness across the system.
