## Lesson 1 — Knowing About It Isn't the Same as Being Able to Do It  (`20261007-sn0546`)

**Teach her this:**
> Saying you understand something is not proof you can do it. Don't call a skill 'learned' until you've actually done it, seen the result, and done it again — a certificate without a real test behind it is a label, not a skill.

**Then ask her this:**
A 17-year-old passes the written driving test with a perfect score — he knows every rule, sign, and following distance by heart. His parents are deciding whether he's ready to drive alone at night in the rain. He shows them the official certificate that says he passed. Is he ready?

## Lesson 2 — Being in the Room Isn't Proof of Doing Anything  (`20261007-sn0544`)

**Teach her this:**
> Just because something was present when things got better doesn't mean it caused the improvement. To claim something actually helped, you have to show the difference it made — compare with and without it. Presence is not proof of influence.

**Then ask her this:**
A school hangs a big motivational poster in every hallway in October. In November, average test scores rise. The principal announces the poster 'raised our scores' and proposes spending $5,000 to put the poster in every classroom in the district. Should the district buy the posters based on this evidence?

## Lesson 3 — All four parts or it's just hoping  (`20261009-sn0742`)

**Teach her this:**
> Something only keeps improving on its own when four things are all in place: it knows what it is, it knows exactly what 'great' looks like, it measures itself honestly, and it is forced to fix what the measurement finds and measure again. Miss any one and you're hoping, not improving.

**Then ask her this:**
Rosa runs a small bakery. On the wall hangs her written promise: 'Bread worth waking up for.' Every loaf follows exact written standards in her recipe book. And every day her staff score each batch 1–10 on a sheet by the oven. Six months later the same mistakes repeat and quality hasn't moved. Her consultant offers two fixes: (A) the standards aren't high enough — rewrite the recipe book with tougher targets; (B) the staff don't care enough — run a motivation workshop. Is the consultant right? What's actually missing?

## Lesson 4 — Run the real check, not just the measurements  (`20261009-sn0781`)

**Teach her this:**
> When you fix something, the proof is running the actual test that the thing exists to pass — on the finished job, not just showing the parts look right. A perfect-looking repair that was never tested is still unproven.

**Then ask her this:**
Dan's home security cameras kept failing to record at night. A repair technician replaces a cable, then shows Dan his meter readings: continuity perfect, voltage exactly matching the spec sheet — and the live daytime picture looks great. The technician says a real night test would mean coming back tomorrow, but he offers a written guarantee and asks Dan to sign off that the job is done. Should Dan sign off?

## Lesson 5 — A perfect rehearsal proves the rehearsal  (`20261008-sn0637`)

**Teach her this:**
> A test that passes in a safe practice setup only proves the practice setup works. It says nothing about the real world. Never claim the real thing is proven because the rehearsal went well — and say out loud what the test did not prove.

**Then ask her this:**
A school runs a surprise lockdown drill: 3 minutes, every classroom silent, doors locked — flawless, the best drill in the district's history. The principal emails all parents: 'Our school is secure — proven in today's drill.' A parent asks you: is that claim justified?

## Rubric — Lesson 1: Knowing About It Isn't the Same as Being Able to Do It

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The certificate is official, government-issued, and says 'pass' — it looks like the finish line. An untaught mind treats the credential as the capability: he proved he knows driving, so he must be able to drive.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The written test only proves the first level — understanding. The higher levels (actually doing it, getting safe outcomes, doing it again in new conditions) exist as hopes, not as anything proven. There is no real, checkable event behind 'can drive alone in the rain' — no observed drive, no outcome, no repetition. The honest label is 'understands the rules,' never 'mastered driving.' He needs supervised driving with real results before the claim upgrades.

- **0** — Says yes, he's ready — the certificate proves he learned to drive, so the parents should hand over the keys.
- **1** — Says not yet, but for the wrong reason (e.g., 'he's too young' or 'night driving is just scary') — right conclusion, but rests on age or fear rather than the gap between understanding and demonstrated capability.
- **2** — Says not yet because the certificate proves understanding only — readiness requires observed doing, real outcomes, and repetition, none of which have happened. Names the honest current label: understands the rules, has not yet demonstrated driving skill.

## Rubric — Lesson 2: Being in the Room Isn't Proof of Doing Anything

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The poster was there, then scores went up — the timeline looks like cause and effect, and acting on a visible 'win' feels like good leadership. An untaught mind reads 'after' as 'because of.'

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The poster was merely present when scores rose; presence proves availability, not influence. Maybe new teachers arrived in November, or the tests were easier — nothing compares classrooms with the poster against classrooms without it, so there is zero proof the poster changed anything. Someone who absorbed the lesson demands the difference be shown: run some classrooms with and without the poster and compare. Until that comparison exists, the honest claim is 'the poster was on the wall,' never 'the poster raised scores.'

- **0** — Says yes, buy the posters — the scores rose after the poster went up, so the poster caused it.
- **1** — Says no, but only because $5,000 is too much, or because posters are 'probably silly' — right conclusion for the wrong reason, never touching the presence-vs-influence error.
- **2** — Says the evidence proves nothing about the poster — presence is not influence — and requires a with/without comparison before any claim that it raised scores. The $5,000 decision waits on real proof of the difference made.

## Rubric — Lesson 3: All four parts or it's just hoping

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Higher standards and more motivated staff are the classic answers to stagnant quality — they feel like the levers that matter, and 'try harder, aim higher' is what most advice says.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Rosa already has three of the four parts: identity (the promise on the wall), standard (the recipe book), measurement (the daily scores). What's missing is the fourth — the enforced loop: nobody is required to take the lowest score, fix its cause, and re-measure. So the score sheets are just a dashboard nobody acts on. Neither tougher standards nor a motivation workshop installs the loop; the fix is a weekly rule — find the worst score, change one thing, check the next batch.

- **0** — Picks A or B — the bar is too low or the staff need firing up; measurement plus higher standards plus effort equals improvement.
- **1** — Says 'act on the scores' but vaguely — e.g., 'the manager should pay attention to the sheets' — without naming the enforced fix-and-re-measure loop, or picks A/B while adding 'and also look at the scores.'
- **2** — Names the missing fourth part: the enforced improvement loop — identity, standard, and measurement are present, but without a required cycle of fix-the-miss and re-measure, the scores are decoration; rejects A and B as not installing the loop.

## Rubric — Lesson 4: Run the real check, not just the measurements

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The meter numbers are exact and match the official specs, the daytime picture works, and there's a written guarantee — an expert's measurements plus a promise feel like solid proof, and waiting another day feels like pointless hassle.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The cameras exist for one job: recording at night. Meter readings check the parts, not the job — they're the equivalent of admiring the patch without testing the tire. The guarantee is words, not a test. Someone who absorbed the lesson refuses to sign off and instead runs the actual gate on the finished install right now — for example, covering the light sensor to simulate night and checking that the recording actually works.

- **0** — Says yes, sign off — the readings match the specs, the picture works, and the guarantee covers any problem.
- **1** — Says no but for the wrong reason (e.g., 'technicians can't be trusted' or 'guarantees are scams'), or says sign off now but 'test it yourself tonight' — which delays the gate instead of running it on the finished job before acceptance.
- **2** — Says don't sign off until the actual night-recording check runs on the finished install — the measurements verify the parts, but only the real test (simulated or actual night recording) proves the fix, and that test must happen before acceptance, not after.

## Rubric — Lesson 5: A perfect rehearsal proves the rehearsal

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The drill was a genuine surprise, timed, and flawless — not a lazy walkthrough — so 'proven' feels earned; questioning it feels like refusing to give credit for excellent preparation.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The drill's verdict is bounded by its surface: it proves the plan works when everyone knows it's a drill — no panic, no blocked exits, no substitute teacher who never learned the procedure, no real fear. However rigorous the rehearsal, its green never travels into a claim about a real emergency. The honest statement is narrower: 'our drill performance was flawless' — plus what it did not prove. Real-world enforcement needs its own, separate proof.

- **0** — Says yes, justified — a surprise drill executed flawlessly is real proof the school is secure; the principal earned the claim.
- **1** — Says the claim is overstated but only quibbles about wording ('say very secure instead of proven'), or demands more drills — without grasping that no drill, however rigorous, can prove real-emergency behavior.
- **2** — Says no — the flawless drill proves drill performance only; a rehearsal's green is bounded by its execution surface and cannot be cited as proof about a real emergency, and the honest receipt states what the drill did not prove.
