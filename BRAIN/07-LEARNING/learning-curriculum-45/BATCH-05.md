## Lesson 1 — Test with what they couldn't figure out alone  (`20261008-sn0636`)

**Teach her this:**
> If you want to prove that your teaching actually works, test it on something the student could never have figured out by themselves. Testing with things they already know — or could work out on their own — proves nothing, no matter how perfect the score.

**Then ask her this:**
Luis invents a new flashcard method and wants to prove it really teaches. First he tests it on his daughter with 20 multiplication facts — she scores 20/20. 'Proof it works!' His wife points out she already knew her times tables. So he re-tests with 20 long-division problems she was never taught — she scores 17/20. 'Now it's proven,' he says. Is it? What should the test use instead?

## Lesson 2 — Lost Notes Are Not Proof  (`20261007-sn0571`)

**Teach her this:**
> A claim is only proven if someone else could re-check the raw evidence it came from — if the raw notes are gone, the result is neither proven nor disproven, it's unknown, and unknown is not a pass. So the rule is: store the raw data somewhere permanent before you announce the result.

**Then ask her this:**
Leo ran a taste test for his science fair project: 20 classmates each tried two cookie recipes, and he says 15 preferred recipe B. The math on his summary is perfect and the test design is sound — but his phone's notes app glitched and wiped the individual tally sheets, so only his summary table survives. He was honest about losing them and asks the teacher to certify his project as proven.

## Lesson 3 — Check That You Actually Wrote It Down  (`20261007-sn0584`)

**Teach her this:**
> Writing things down isn't enough — you have to check that you actually wrote them down. If someone tells you something important and you can't show where it landed, it vanished into thin air. So run a regular check: 'they said these things — where did each one go?' For anything missing, there are only three honest answers: it landed somewhere else, it's queued, or you genuinely missed it — and a genuine miss means you capture it late, tell the person directly, and write down why it slipped.

**Then ask her this:**
You run a small catering business. Clients call with wishes — allergies, delivery times, menu swaps — and your two staff scribble them on whatever paper is nearby. Last Saturday a client's 'no nuts — her grandson is allergic' note never reached the kitchen, and there was a hospital scare. The staff say: 'We wrote it down, it just got lost.' Your current fix is a shared notebook by the phone. Is that enough, and what do you do about the wishes that already slipped through?

## Lesson 4 — Check the Checker First  (`20261007-sn0570`)

**Teach her this:**
> When something fails right after the rules changed, don't blame the thing being checked first — compare what the checker expects against what the new rules actually require. If the checker is outdated, the failure describes the checker's age, not the product's health.

**Then ask her this:**
A school adds a new unit to the science syllabus mid-year and updates the textbook. The auto-grader's answer key was never updated — it still covers the old syllabus. A student, Ana, answers the new-unit questions correctly according to the new textbook, but the auto-grader marks them wrong and her report shows a failing grade. The teacher must decide: make Ana retake the exam, or fix the answer key.

## Lesson 5 — A Check Must Actually Run the Thing  (`20261007-sn0569`)

**Teach her this:**
> A safety check that only confirms something exists doesn't prove it works. To guard anything, the check has to actually exercise it — press the button, run it, listen for the sound — not just tick a box that says it's there.

**Then ask her this:**
A landlord's management company does a monthly safety inspection: the inspector walks past each apartment's smoke alarm and ticks a box on a form labeled 'alarm present.' The reports come back green every month for a year, and the landlord cites them as proof the alarms are maintained. Then a kitchen fire breaks out in unit 4B — its alarm never sounds because the battery is dead and the sensor is clogged. The tenant asks: did a year of green inspections mean the alarms worked, and what should the inspection actually do?

## Rubric — Lesson 1: Test with what they couldn't figure out alone

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The second test feels like the honest fix — brand-new material she was never taught, and a strong score on it looks exactly like proof the method works.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Long division is derivable: a clever kid who knows multiplication can work out division on her own, so the 17/20 can't separate what the flashcards taught from what her own reasoning produced. The test still can't distinguish the method from nothing. The lesson demands non-derivable material — something she could not possibly work out alone, like French vocabulary words, where each answer is arbitrary. Only a pass there proves the method taught something.

- **0** — Says yes, proven — new material, never taught, strong score; the wife's objection was already handled by the second test.
- **1** — Senses something is still off but can't name it ('maybe test more problems' or 'maybe she guessed'), or suggests harder division — still derivable material, missing the derivability point.
- **2** — Says not proven — long division can be derived from what she already knows, so the score can't separate the method from her own reasoning; the test must use non-derivable material (like arbitrary vocabulary) that she could never work out alone.

## Rubric — Lesson 2: Lost Notes Are Not Proof

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Everything checks out — the math is right, the design is sound, nobody thinks Leo lied — so the tempting call is to certify it. Punishing an honest kid for a glitch feels wrong, and the numbers look real.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Someone who absorbed the lesson refuses both 'proven' and 'you're lying.' The math being right proves the arithmetic, not that 15 kids actually picked recipe B, that the two groups were fairly run, or that there weren't extra trials Leo forgot to mention. With the tally sheets gone, the claim is uncheckable — neither confirmed nor refuted — and an uncheckable claim can't be certified. The honest verdict is inconclusive: praise the design, withhold the certification, and have him rerun it keeping the sheets this time.

- **0** — The teacher certifies the project as proven ('the math checks out, good enough') — or she accuses Leo of faking it. Either way she treated missing evidence as evidence.
- **1** — She reaches the right verdict (won't certify) but for the wrong reason — e.g., 'rules say sheets or fail, no exceptions' without understanding why — or she understands the evidence is gone but still certifies it 'because Leo is honest.'
- **2** — She rules the claim inconclusive — neither proven nor disproven — because without the raw tally sheets nobody can re-check what was observed; she withholds certification and has Leo rerun the test with the sheets kept somewhere permanent.

## Rubric — Lesson 3: Check That You Actually Wrote It Down

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The notebook feels like the fix — 'now we write everything in one place' — and the instinct is to blame the staff's carelessness. An untaught mind thinks capture IS the solution, and treats the already-lost notes as gone and unfixable.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The failure was never that nothing was written — it was that nobody ever verified each incoming wish landed somewhere the kitchen actually reads. The notebook needs a closing loop: a daily check comparing what clients said (the call log, the messages) against what the kitchen plan shows, asking 'they said these things — where did each one go?' Each item gets one of three honest answers: it landed somewhere else, it's queued, or it was genuinely missed. The notebook alone is step one, not the fix. And the already-lost wishes aren't history — for each genuine miss, you go back to the client, capture it properly now, tell them directly what happened, and write down why it slipped so the cause gets fixed, not just the symptom.

- **0** — The notebook alone, plus 'staff must be more careful' — treats writing it down as the whole fix and writes off the lost notes.
- **1** — Adds a check ('we'll review the notebook weekly') but with no closed answers for each item, or handles new wishes while ignoring what to do about the ones already lost.
- **2** — Applies the lesson: a regular audit — 'they said X, where did it land?' — with exactly three honest answers per item (landed elsewhere, queued, genuine miss); and for genuine misses: capture late, tell the affected person directly, and log the cause.

## Rubric — Lesson 4: Check the Checker First

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The machine says 'wrong' in red, so the tempting move is to trust it and conclude Ana doesn't know the material — make her study and retake. Questioning the machine feels like making excuses for the student.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Someone who absorbed the lesson holds the two things side by side before touching either: what the answer key expects versus what the current syllabus requires. The mismatch lives in the key, not in Ana's answers — the key is grading yesterday's syllabus. So the failing grade describes the key's age, not Ana's knowledge: the fix belongs to the answer key, and Ana's correct answers stand.

- **0** — The teacher makes Ana retake the exam or 'fixes' her answers — she patched the product for the checker's debt.
- **1** — She fixes the answer key but can't say why beyond 'the machine must be wrong sometimes' — or she correctly spots the key is outdated yet still makes Ana retake 'to be safe.'
- **2** — She explicitly compares the key's expectations against the current syllabus, names the failure as the checker's (outdated key), fixes the key, and lets Ana's correct answers stand — with reasoning that the red marks measured the key's age, not the student's knowledge.

## Rubric — Lesson 5: A Check Must Actually Run the Thing

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** A year of green monthly reports is tempting proof — the company checked every single month, so the dead battery looks like bad luck, not a failed inspection. Nobody thinks to blame the checklist.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Someone who absorbed the lesson sees that the inspection only ever proved presence, never function — it never pressed the test button, so a dead alarm and a live one looked identical to it. A year of green reports is proof the alarms existed, not that they worked. A real inspection has to actually run the alarm — press the test button and listen — because a guard's claim is limited to what it actually exercises.

- **0** — The tenant (or landlord) concludes the green reports prove the alarms were fine and the fire was just bad luck — trusting the green without asking what the check actually did.
- **1** — They realize the alarms should be tested but can't say why the reports failed — e.g., 'inspections should be more thorough' in general — or they correctly demand a button-press test yet still treat the past green year as evidence the alarms were probably working.
- **2** — They conclude the green year proved only that the alarms were present, not working — because the check never exercised them — and say the inspection must actually run each alarm (press the test button, confirm it sounds) to claim anything about safety.
