## Lesson 1 — Fix the Real Thing, Not the Practice Copy  (`20261007-sn0548`)

**Teach her this:**
> If your test copy and the real thing share the same flaw, fixing the test copy fixes nothing. The repair has to land on the version people actually use — a fix in the practice version is just an experiment, never proof the real problem is gone.

**Then ask her this:**
A bakery chain develops recipes in a small test kitchen, then bakes from a printed 'production recipe card' at every shop. The new scone recipe has too much salt — in both the test-kitchen recipe and the production card. The head baker fixes the test-kitchen recipe, bakes a perfect test batch, and is about to announce the problem solved. Is it solved?

## Lesson 2 — After a Big Reorganization, Check the Insides  (`20261008-sn0633`)

**Teach her this:**
> When you reorganize everything at once, the normal checks only tell you everything is still there — they can't tell you anything important got hollowed out inside. After a big reorganization, open the important pieces and check their insides on the finished result.

**Then ask her this:**
A school moves 500 student records into a new office system over the weekend. Monday morning the IT manager reports: all 500 files transferred, zero errors, the new system opens fine. The principal must certify to the district that every student's record is intact. What does she do before signing?

## Lesson 3 — Your Knowledge Is Yours, Not the App's  (`20261007-sn0617`)

**Teach her this:**
> Your notes, memories, and records belong to you — not to whatever app or AI you typed them into. The helper is interchangeable; your intelligence is the constant.

**Then ask her this:**
A freelance bookkeeper keeps all her clients' financial history, quirks, and deadlines inside one AI accounting app's 'smart memory.' The app announces a big price hike. She's terrified to leave: 'No other tool knows my clients like this one does.' What should she understand?

## Lesson 4 — Sell the wonder, not the money  (`20261009-sn0744`)

**Teach her this:**
> Don't lead with promises of money to win people over — make something so delightful and useful that people love using it, and the money follows on its own as a side effect. Leading with money attracts the wrong crowd and the wrong behavior.

**Then ask her this:**
Maya's custom-cake business is slow. A marketing advisor tells her to run ads saying: 'Custom cakes for 40% less than the big bakeries — keep your money in your pocket!' Her friend disagrees: 'Post close-up videos of your cakes — the colors, the moment the knife cuts in — make people drool.' Maya can only afford one campaign. Which should she choose, and why?

## Lesson 5 — Hide the machinery  (`20261009-sn0743`)

**Teach her this:**
> People should feel wonder using what you built without needing to understand how it works. All the clever machinery stays on the builder's side. If the user needs a manual or an explanation, the presentation failed.

**Then ask her this:**
Lena built a simple budgeting app for her 78-year-old mother. Design A shows spending categories, charts, 'smart categorization engine' badges, a sync status line, and a short tutorial video explaining how the predictions work. Design B shows one screen: 'You have $340 left for groceries this month,' in a calm color — nothing else. Lena is proud of the engine and believes showing it proves the app is smart and trustworthy. Her mother once asked, 'But how does it know?' Which design should Lena ship?

## Rubric — Lesson 1: Fix the Real Thing, Not the Practice Copy

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The test batch tastes right, which feels like proof the fix works — and the temptation is to declare victory, since the recipe was 'fixed' and verified delicious. Closing it out looks efficient.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The test kitchen is the instrument, not the machine. Every shop still bakes from the salty production card, so the real flaw is untouched — the good test batch only proves the new salt level works, which is an experiment, not a repair. Someone who absorbed the lesson asks 'where does the fix actually run?' and updates the production recipe card at every shop before claiming the problem is solved, then verifies a shop batch.

- **0** — Says yes, it's solved — the test batch proved the fix, so no further action is needed. Lets the shops keep baking from the salty card.
- **1** — Says the production card should also be updated, but treats the test-batch success as the proof the problem is closed — right instinct, wrong claim about what the test batch established.
- **2** — Says no — the test-kitchen fix is only an experiment; the repair must land on the production recipe card the shops actually use, and closure requires a shop batch baked from the corrected card.

## Rubric — Lesson 2: After a Big Reorganization, Check the Insides

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** All the numbers say green — 500 moved, zero errors, system works. The tempting conclusion: the migration succeeded and signing off is just paperwork.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** File counts and a working system prove presence, not intactness — a bulk move can silently drop the inner fields (allergy plans, custody orders, required signatures) while every file looks present and accounted for. The lesson says the move's own green is never the acceptance. She opens a sample of the important records in the new system and verifies every required field inside is still there — on the finished result — before certifying.

- **0** — Signs off on the IT report — 500 files moved, zero errors, system opens; that's proof enough.
- **1** — Runs a deeper check of the wrong thing (re-runs the transfer, checks the old system has backups) or opens a few records by name only without verifying their inner fields; or starts the right check but signs off before it finishes.
- **2** — Withholds certification until the finished records are opened and their required inner fields verified on the new system — and can say why the transfer's own green report doesn't count.

## Rubric — Lesson 3: Your Knowledge Is Yours, Not the App's

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Three years of client history live inside the app — it feels like the app holds the knowledge. Leaving means starting over, so the price hike feels like a ransom she has to pay.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The knowledge is hers — the client histories, the quirks, the deadlines. The app was just the current helper holding it. The lesson separates the two: keep the intelligence in a form that belongs to her (her own records), and the tool becomes interchangeable — any competent app can work from her records. She exports her data into her own system first; then switching is a shopping decision, not a hostage situation.

- **0** — Stays and pays — leaving would mean losing the client knowledge; the app 'knows' her clients.
- **1** — Leaves for a cheaper app but abandons the three years of history (starts over), or exports the data but still believes the new app needs months to 'learn' her clients before it works.
- **2** — Exports her records so the knowledge stays hers, then chooses any tool freely — explaining that the intelligence and the tool were never the same thing.

## Rubric — Lesson 4: Sell the wonder, not the money

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The savings pitch speaks directly to what everyone cares about — money — and when business is slow, promising savings feels like the honest, rational move; drool-worthy videos feel like vanity.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Leading with savings frames Maya as the cheap option: it attracts bargain hunters who haggle and vanish, forces her to compete on price, and makes every sale a transaction about cost. Leading with delight frames her as the extraordinary option: people pay a premium for wonder, they come back, they tell friends. The income arrives as the byproduct of the awesomeness — exactly the ordering the lesson demands — while the money-first pitch optimizes for the effect directly and gets the extractive version of it.

- **0** — Chooses the savings ads — when money is tight you talk about money; promising 40% off is the fastest way to get customers.
- **1** — Chooses the videos but for shallow reasons ('pretty pictures get more clicks'), or chooses the savings ads but says she'll 'add some nice photos too' — right instinct about presentation without grasping that leading with money changes the frame and the customer.
- **2** — Chooses the delight campaign with the reasoning that leading with money frames her as cheap, attracts hagglers, and starts a price race to the bottom — while leading with the wonderful experience makes income arrive as its byproduct, from customers who pay for wonder and return.

## Rubric — Lesson 5: Hide the machinery

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Showing the work feels honest and impressive — hiding it feels like dumbing things down or even tricking her mother; and since Mom asked how it works, explaining seems like respecting her.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The engine, the badges, and the tutorial are builder's pride leaking into the product — every visible layer of machinery is a tax on the person using it. Mom doesn't need to understand the predictions to benefit from them, any more than she needs to understand electricity to use a lamp. The right answer to 'how does it know?' is a product so clear the question never arises. Ship B: one true, useful sentence, wonder without explanation — and keep the cleverness in the code where it belongs.

- **0** — Ships A — transparency builds trust, the tutorial respects Mom's intelligence, and showing the engine proves the app is genuinely smart.
- **1** — Ships B but only because 'old people can't handle complexity,' or ships A with the tutorial removed — right surface, wrong reason (condescension instead of the principle that machinery belongs to builders).
- **2** — Ships B with the reasoning that the machinery is for the builder, not the user — if Mom needs the tutorial to use it, the presentation failed; value requires no understanding, and pride in the engine stays in the code.
