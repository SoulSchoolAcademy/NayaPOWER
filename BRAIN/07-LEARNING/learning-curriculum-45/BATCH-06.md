## Lesson 1 — Name Exactly What Each Win Proves  (`20261007-sn0555`)

**Teach her this:**
> A win at one level is not a win at the next: 'it runs' is not 'it works in a trial,' and 'it works in a trial' is not 'it works in the real world.' Name exactly what each success proves — and a refusal to certify beyond what was proven is the safeguard working, not a failure.

**Then ask her this:**
A friend develops a new soup recipe. First win: the recipe card has no typos and reads clearly. Second win: she cooks it in her test kitchen and tasters love it. She announces: 'Proven — this soup will be a hit in every restaurant.' The food-safety board declines to certify it for commercial sale until it's tested in a real commercial kitchen; she calls the refusal a failure of the process. Two questions: does her announcement hold up, and is the board's refusal a failure?

## Lesson 2 — The Draft Is Not the Finished Thing  (`20261007-sn0563`)

**Teach her this:**
> Showing the draft you sent doesn't prove it made it into the finished product. The only proof is finding it inside the finished thing itself — especially when the landing process can change what you sent.

**Then ask her this:**
A cookbook author emails recipe draft v3 to her editor, who replies 'added to the book!' The editor is known to lightly rewrite every recipe during layout — shortening steps, renumbering, retitling. Months later, the author is asked to prove her recipe is in the published edition. She holds up her sent draft v3 and the editor's 'added!' reply. A colleague says that's not proof — she must find the actual recipe inside the printed book. Who's right?

## Lesson 3 — The Headline Can Lie — Read the Steps  (`20261008-sn0629`)

**Teach her this:**
> A big red FAILED or a green SUCCESS at the top of a report can be wrong — in either direction. Never trust the headline; read each step and decide for yourself.

**Then ask her this:**
A school runs its fire drill. Minutes later the automated safety system sends the principal a red alert: DRILL STATUS: FAILED. The district requires a report by 3pm. What's the principal's correct move?

## Lesson 4 — A Broken Machine Is Not a Guilty Verdict  (`20261007-sn0549`)

**Teach her this:**
> If the testing tool breaks down halfway through the test, that breakdown says nothing about the person or thing being tested. Fix the tool (re-run the test) and judge only the clean result — never grade someone on the machine's crash.

**Then ask her this:**
A teacher gives her class a spelling quiz through an online auto-grader. Halfway through one student's quiz, the school Wi-Fi drops, the app freezes mid-word, and the screen flashes 'FAILED' before kicking the student out. The teacher has to enter a grade. What goes in the grade book?

## Lesson 5 — Take a Fresh Reading Before You Treat a Stale One  (`20261007-sn0554`)

**Teach her this:**
> A failure or warning is only true about the moment it was measured. Before you act on it, check the current state first — if the problem is already gone, the right move is to do nothing, not to "fix" something that isn't broken anymore.

**Then ask her this:**
On Monday, your car's dashboard showed a low-tire-pressure warning light, and you snapped a photo of it. On Tuesday, your partner took the car to the shop, had the leaking tire repaired, and the light went off. On Wednesday, you come across the Monday photo of the warning light and are about to book another shop appointment to fix the tire. Should you book the appointment?

## Rubric — Lesson 1: Name Exactly What Each Win Proves

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Tasters loved it — that feels like real proof, and the board's refusal feels like bureaucracy blocking a good thing. Promoting the test-kitchen win into a real-world claim is the natural victory lap.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Someone who absorbed the lesson labels each win for exactly what it earned: the clean card proves it reads well, the test kitchen proves it works in a trial — neither proves it will succeed in real restaurants, which is a higher level needing its own evidence. So her announcement overclaims by two levels. And the board's refusal isn't a failure — it's the safeguard doing its job, refusing to let a lower-level win be spent as a higher-level claim.

- **0** — Her announcement is accepted ('tasters loved it, so it'll work in restaurants') and/or the board's refusal is treated as a failure or bureaucratic obstruction.
- **1** — They correctly reject the announcement but only vaguely ('you can't be sure about restaurants') without naming what each win actually proved — or they defend the board's refusal yet still let her say 'proven' about the test kitchen as if it covered restaurants.
- **2** — They name each win's exact level (reads well / works in a trial — neither is real-world proof), reject the announcement as overclaiming, and recognize the board's refusal as the safeguard working correctly — with reasoning that a green at one level never buys a claim at the next.

## Rubric — Lesson 2: The Draft Is Not the Finished Thing

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The draft matches, the editor confirmed it in writing — demanding she dig through the printed book feels like busywork and distrust. The sent file looks like solid evidence.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Someone who absorbed the lesson sides with the colleague. The sent draft is the pre-landing version — and because the editor rewrites recipes on the way in, the draft is not what landed; comparing it to the book proves nothing, since the landed version may look different. The only proof of inclusion is locating the recipe inside the finished book itself.

- **0** — The draft and the editor's reply are accepted as proof — the pre-landing version is treated as the landed thing.
- **1** — She checks the printed book but for the wrong reason — e.g., 'the editor might be lying' — missing that the rewrite-on-landing is what kills the draft as evidence; or she agrees the book must be checked yet still cites the draft as backup proof.
- **2** — She finds the recipe inside the published book to prove inclusion, with reasoning that the sent draft is only the pre-landing version and — because landing rewrites it — can never prove what's in the finished book.

## Rubric — Lesson 3: The Headline Can Lie — Read the Steps

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The alert says FAILED in red letters. The obvious response: report the drill as failed, apologize, schedule a full re-drill, maybe discipline the safety officer.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The headline is a summary, not a measurement. She opens the drill's step log: evacuation completed in 3 minutes (pass), headcount complete (pass) — the only failure is the final step, where the system couldn't upload the report to the district portal because the Wi-Fi dropped. The drill itself worked; the failure is a reporting artifact. She reports the drill as successful, fixes the filing step, and doesn't waste everyone's time on a re-drill.

- **0** — Inherits the headline — reports the drill as failed and schedules a full re-drill.
- **1** — Reads the steps and finds the Wi-Fi filing failure, but still reports the drill as failed (right reasoning, wrong conclusion), or orders a re-drill anyway 'to be safe.'
- **2** — Reads the steps, identifies the failure as the reporting step only, reports the drill as successful with the filing fixed — no re-drill.

## Rubric — Lesson 4: A Broken Machine Is Not a Guilty Verdict

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The system produced a verdict — 'FAILED' in bold, logged in the system — and recording it looks diligent and consistent. An untaught mind treats the machine's output as the verdict, since the machine is supposed to be impartial.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The Wi-Fi dropout is an instrument failure, not a student result. The 'FAILED' describes the crashed session, the same way a blackout mid-bake says nothing about the cake. The teacher must not classify that death as a grade. The right move is to re-run the quiz under working conditions (Wi-Fi stable, fresh session) and grade only the clean run — the crash goes in the log as noise, never in the grade book.

- **0** — Records the F (or a failing/low grade) from the crashed session, or averages it in — treats the machine's breakdown as a verdict on the student.
- **1** — Discards the F but replaces it with a guess (e.g., 'she probably knew it, give her a B') or excuses the student without re-testing — right to reject the crash, but replaces a verdict with a guess instead of a clean measurement.
- **2** — Refuses to grade the crashed session, re-runs the quiz under working conditions, and grades only the clean result — names the Wi-Fi drop as an instrument event, not evidence about the student.

## Rubric — Lesson 5: Take a Fresh Reading Before You Treat a Stale One

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The photo looks like live evidence of a real problem, and booking the appointment feels responsible — ignoring a warning light seems reckless. An untaught mind treats the photo as current fact and acts on it immediately.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The photo is evidence about Monday, not Wednesday — evidence carries its timestamp. Before spending money and disturbing a fixed car, take a fresh reading: check the live tire gauge or glance at the dashboard today. If the pressure is fine and the light is off, the Monday red is a stale condition, not a repair target. The correct, complete action is to cancel the appointment and change nothing.

- **0** — Books the shop appointment based on the Monday photo, or says the tire still needs fixing — treats stale evidence as current without any fresh check.
- **1** — Says to skip the appointment but only because 'the partner probably fixed it' (guess, not a fresh reading), or says to check the tire but still books the appointment 'to be safe' despite a clean reading.
- **2** — Takes a fresh reading first (checks the gauge/dashboard today), concludes the Monday warning no longer describes the car, and cancels the appointment — explains that doing nothing is the correct repair because the problem no longer exists.
