## Lesson 1 — Every Report Is a Photo, Not a Live Feed  (`20261007-sn0564`)

**Teach her this:**
> Any 'here's the current situation' message was true when it was written — but it starts going stale the second it's sent, through nobody's fault. Before you act on one, check the live situation yourself and update your plan to match.

**Then ask her this:**
At 2pm, Lena's friend — a real estate agent who toured a building that morning — texts: 'The apartment at 12 Birch Street is still available; the landlord will hold it if you wire the $1,000 deposit today.' Lena trusts her completely; the information was accurate when sent. It's now 6pm and Lena is about to wire the money from her phone to lock it in. Should she send it based on the text, or do something first?

## Lesson 2 — If You Didn't Do It, Don't Let the Record Say You Did  (`20261007-sn0553`)

**Teach her this:**
> If you promised to do something but someone else did it before you got there, correct the record openly. Never leave a note, claim, or receipt standing that makes it look like you did work you didn't do — even when nobody would ever find out.

**Then ask her this:**
You volunteer in the neighborhood group chat to set up the chairs before Saturday's block party, and you write 'I'll handle the chairs.' When you arrive, a neighbor you don't know has already set up every chair. Later, people in the chat start thanking you for the chairs. What should you write in the group chat?

## Lesson 3 — A Lesson Isn't Learned Until It's Used  (`20261008-sn0628`)

**Teach her this:**
> Writing a lesson down — even getting it approved — isn't learning. A lesson only counts as learned when someone actually uses it and it changes what happens, and you verify the change.

**Then ask her this:**
A family restaurant keeps overcooking steaks — the third customer complaint this month. The owner holds a training, writes a new temperature procedure, and everyone signs it. He tells a regular: 'We learned our lesson.' Has the lesson been learned?

## Lesson 4 — Writers Can't Award Their Own Badges  (`20261007-sn0581`)

**Teach her this:**
> Writing something down doesn't make it true — and the person doing the writing must never be the one who gets to stamp it approved. Everything that enters the record starts at the lowest trust level, and only the proper authority's own action can raise it. A polite request to 'please be honest' is not enforcement; the system itself has to make the stamp impossible for the writer to give.

**Then ask her this:**
You run a neighborhood tool-lending app. Members list their own tools, and items carry a 'SAFETY TESTED' badge that's supposed to mean a qualified inspector checked the tool. To make signup fast, the listing form includes a checkbox — filled in by the tool's OWNER — that says 'This tool passed safety testing.' Most members check it honestly. One member's untested chainsaw goes out with the badge on it, and a borrower is hurt. The founder's first instinct is to add a warning under the checkbox: 'Please only check this if the tool was really tested.' What's actually wrong, and what's the real fix?

## Lesson 5 — When Two Things Share a Name, the Master List Decides — Rename, Never Erase  (`20261007-sn0545`)

**Teach her this:**
> If two things end up with the same name, the official master list decides which one keeps it. Fix the mix-up by giving the other one its own name — never delete one (that destroys the evidence of the mistake), never let both share the name, and the person who spotted it reports it while the rightful owner does the renaming.

**Then ask her this:**
A school office discovers there are two students named Maya Chen — one in the 4th grade, one in the 6th — but the official class roster lists only one Maya Chen. Report cards, bus lists, and permission slips are starting to go to the wrong child. The office clerk has three options on the table: delete one record as a 'duplicate,' add a second identical 'Maya Chen' entry, or just decide herself which one gets a middle initial. What should the office actually do?

## Rubric — Lesson 1: Every Report Is a Photo, Not a Live Feed

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The text is from a trusted expert, it was correct hours ago, and speed matters — someone else could grab the apartment. Verifying first feels like distrusting a good friend and risking the deal.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Someone who absorbed the lesson knows trust isn't the issue — time is. The 2pm text was a photograph of the situation at 2pm, not a live feed; four hours of other applicants could have changed everything. Wiring money is consequential, so before acting she must look at the live state herself — check the listing, call the landlord — and re-aim her plan at what she finds. If it's gone, the text wasn't wrong; it was just a photo.

- **0** — Lena wires the deposit based on the text alone — she acted on a photograph as if it were a live feed.
- **1** — She checks the listing first but only out of distrust ('my friend might be lying') rather than understanding that true-when-sent information decays — or she checks, finds it still available, and concludes the check was unnecessary.
- **2** — She verifies the live state herself before wiring — checking the listing or calling the landlord — with reasoning that the text was true when sent but decays in transit, and that a consequential action requires a fresh look, not a four-hour-old photo.

## Rubric — Lesson 2: If You Didn't Do It, Don't Let the Record Say You Did

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Nobody saw the neighbor, and letting the thanks roll in costs nothing and makes you look good. An untaught mind stays quiet — the original message already reads as if you did it, so silence is the easy, profitable move.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The message 'I'll handle the chairs' was a promise about the future, and the future changed: the job was already done by another hand. Leaving the original message uncorrected turns a promise into a false claim of credit. The lesson demands a public correction, in the same place the promise landed: 'The chairs were already set up when I arrived — not by me. Credit belongs to whoever got here first.' One correction costs a little ego and buys a permanently honest record.

- **0** — Stays silent and accepts the thanks, or writes something like 'happy to help' — lets the record credit them for work they didn't do.
- **1** — Privately tells one person they didn't do it, but leaves the group-chat record standing uncorrected; or corrects it but implies they shared the work ('we got it done') without evidence they lifted a chair.
- **2** — Posts a correction in the group chat stating the chairs were already done by someone else, disclaims authorship plainly, and does not take credit — the record now says exactly what happened.

## Rubric — Lesson 3: A Lesson Isn't Learned Until It's Used

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Training done, procedure written, signatures collected — that feels like learning. The paperwork says the lesson is closed.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** Approving a lesson is just words becoming official — active on paper. The lesson says learning only proves itself when it's applied: the next steaks must actually come out right, consistently, and someone must check. Until behavior changes and the change is verified, the training produced a document, not learning. The honest answer: 'We've written the lesson; we'll know it's learned when fifty straight steaks come out right.'

- **0** — Yes — training completed, procedure signed, lesson learned. Closes the book.
- **1** — Says 'let's wait and see' without naming what evidence would prove it, or checks one good steak and declares victory; or correctly reasons that behavior must change but still concludes the signed procedure proves the lesson was learned.
- **2** — Says not yet — the lesson counts as learned only when applied behavior is observed and verified over time, and names the evidence (e.g., steaks consistently right).

## Rubric — Lesson 4: Writers Can't Award Their Own Badges

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** The warning feels like the responsible fix — and blaming the dishonest member feels like justice. An untaught mind treats this as a honesty problem to be solved with a reminder, never noticing that the form itself hands the stamp to the person with the incentive to claim it.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** The checkbox lets the writer mint the authority — the badge is awarded by the same person who benefits from claiming it, so a warning is just convention the write path can ignore. The real fix makes the write path authority-blind: members' listings enter with no badge by default, mechanically, and only the inspector's own action — their own login, their own stamp — can ever set it. And the same rule has to cover every other trust mark in the app: identity-verified, insured, whatever comes next. The authority travels with the authority's action, never with the writer's form.

- **0** — Adds the warning, or punishes the member — leaves the form able to mint the badge (convention instead of enforcement).
- **1** — Removes the checkbox but never installs the inspector-side authority — e.g., deletes the badge entirely, or says 'inspectors will check later' without making the badge mechanically inspector-only.
- **2** — Applies the lesson: the write path is authority-blind — listings enter at the lowest trust state by default, the badge can only ever be set by the inspector's own action (enforced by design, not honesty), and the same rule covers every other trust mark.

## Rubric — Lesson 5: When Two Things Share a Name, the Master List Decides — Rename, Never Erase

**Trap (what the untaught mind does):** Deleting the 'extra' record looks like tidy, decisive data cleanup — and the clerk deciding herself looks helpful and fast. An untaught mind picks the quickest repair: erase the duplicate or let one person settle it.

**Lesson-applied reasoning (what a 2 looks like):** This is an identity collision, not a duplicate to delete. The official roster is the authority — the Maya Chen already on it keeps the name, exactly as listed. Deleting one record would erase the evidence of the collision and could destroy the wrong child's history; adding a second identical entry makes every future mix-up permanent; and the clerk who spotted it has the authority to report, not to rename someone else's record. The repair: report the collision, and let each student's family (the owners) help assign a proper distinguishing identifier through the school's official process.

- **0** — Deletes one record as a duplicate, or registers both under the identical name 'Maya Chen,' or lets the clerk unilaterally rename one — picks a forbidden repair: erasure, double-entry, or overreach.
- **1** — Rejects deletion but leaves both children sharing the name with no distinguishing identifier, or has the roster updated by the clerk alone without the families — avoids the worst repair but doesn't resolve the identity collision through the right authority.
- **2** — Treats the roster as the authority (listed Maya keeps the name), resolves the collision by assigning a distinct identifier through the proper owners, never deletes, never double-registers, and keeps the finder's role as reporter rather than renamer.
